# The River

On a quiet Sunday morning in June, Minh walked by the river with Rose Mai. She was seven now. She carried Grandma Rose's spyglass everywhere.

They walked along the path from the old bridge to the castle. It was the same path Minh had walked hundreds of times: with Sam, with Hana, with Grandma Rose in her wheelchair, alone on difficult days. The river was clear and fast. Salmon jumped near the weir.

"Papa," said Rose Mai, "tell me the story again."

"Which story?"

"The story of you. When you came."

So Minh told her. The boy from a small town by a river in Vietnam. The scholarship. The lady and the gentleman at the station. The kiss on the cheek. "Now you are a member of this family."

"That was Grandma Rose," said Rose Mai.

"That was Grandma Rose."

They sat on a bench, the one with a small brass plaque: "For Rose, who loved this river." Rose Mai looked through the spyglass at the castle.

Minh thought about the town. It had changed so much in twelve years. Riverford was a tight-knit community, but no longer a closed one. A hyper-local newspaper had become a national example. A café had become a community-owned institution. A wholly-owned subsidiary of a big company had tried to buy the high street and failed. Ex-offenders like Andrei had found work through a victim-offender program, reconciliation-based, that the town now ran for others.

He thought about the bigger world. Climate change was a double-edged problem: dangerous, but also a chance to build better towns. Some people had become eco-refugees, forced from their homes by floods and heat. A few had come to Riverford. The town had welcomed them, mostly. There were still self-defeating arguments, still razor-thin votes in the council. But the direction was clear.

The town was greener. Emission-restricted streets in the centre, an ultra-low-emission bus fleet, turbine-generated power from the hills. Hassle-free bikes everywhere. Fewer cars waiting bumper-to-bumper on the old bridge. High-occupancy lanes for shared cars. Go-slows by angry drivers had stopped. The heat-trapping gases from the town had fallen by half.

Food had changed too. The school served nutrient-dense, nutrient-fortified meals, no empty-calorie snacks. The hospital had a non-communicable disease program for heart and diabetes care, so people stayed well, not just got treated when ill. A national debate about a single-payer health system and cradle-to-grave care continued. Belt-tightening times came and went.

The Gazette was in good hands. Minh had trained three young journalists from the Sunday club. They were computer-savvy, well-versed in data, brave. One wanted to be a truth-teller, she said, "like Minh." Minh laughed and said she would be better than him. Her tech-savviness was already far beyond his.

A micro-influencer from the town, a teenage girl, had more followers than the Gazette's website. Minh didn't mind. She used her platform to talk about the river. That was good enough. Self-quizzing apps helped students in the Sunday club. Co-curricular programs at the school taught children to be self-diagnosing about their own learning. Van-pooling for workers had reduced traffic. Double-earner families got government-subsidised childcare. Non-custodial sentences for small crimes had replaced short prison terms. The new museum, in a beaux-arts style building, showed the excavation-based history of the town. It was hub-centred: everything connected to it.

Not all ideas worked. A laissez-faire approach to the town's old market had failed; it nearly closed. Near-monopolies in online shopping still hurt small shops. A non-tariff trade rule from abroad had damaged local farms. Norm-referenced exams still stressed children. And attention-grabbing headlines on national websites still spread fear faster than facts. Multi-faceted problems, Minh thought. No single answer.

But then, in biology, Leo had told him, life started as single-celled organisms, and look what happened next. Things grow. Things change. Things get better, slowly, if people work at it.

"Papa," said Rose Mai, lowering the spyglass. "Why do rivers always go to the sea?"

Minh thought about it. He had asked himself the same question at her age, sitting by another river, on the other side of the world.

"Because they're looking for something bigger," he said. "And when they find it, they don't stop being rivers. They just become part of something bigger."

Rose Mai thought about that. "Like you," she said. "You came from Vietnam and became part of here. But you're still Vietnamese."

Minh looked at his daughter. Seven years old, and she understood everything, even the non-linguistic things: the feelings under the words.

"Yes," he said. "Exactly like that."

They sat by the river for a long time, the father and the daughter, the old town around them and the castle on the hill. The water ran past them, as it always had, as it always would, toward the sea.

Then Rose Mai stood up. "Let's go home," she said. "I want to make Grandma's cake."

"Will it be burnt?"

"Probably," said Rose Mai. "But only a little."

And they walked home together along the river, toward the house where a family was waiting.

The end.
