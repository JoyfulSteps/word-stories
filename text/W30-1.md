# The Reunion

In July, Minh graduated. And for the **occasion**, his parents came to England. It was their first time **overseas**. They had never been on a plane before.

Minh waited at the airport for two hours. When he saw them come through the doors, he could not speak. His mother looked smaller than he remembered. His father looked older. They were carrying three huge bags of food. "Because English food is bad," his mother said, before she even said hello. Then she cried and held him for a long time.

It was a **reunion** Minh would never forget.

The Parkers organized a big **gathering** to welcome them. Everyone came: Grandma Rose, Margaret, Daniel, Pat, Sam, Hana, Mr. Okafor, Grace from the Gazette. Anna cooked a huge **banquet**. Minh's mother cooked too, in Anna's kitchen, and the two mothers argued happily about salt.

Minh had to interpret for his parents. They spoke very little English. Tom spoke no Vietnamese. "I'm the interpreter," Minh said. "It's not just words. It's the intonation too: how you say things. It's harder than translating. You have to do it immediately." But sometimes no communication was needed. Tom and Minh's father went into the garage together to look at an old motorbike. They came out an hour later, covered in oil and laughing. They had no common language, but they could communicate perfectly.

Grandma Rose and Minh's mother had a long conversation, with Minh in the middle. They talked about their sons, their husbands, their lives. Grandma Rose was moved. "Tell her she has a wonderful son," she said. Minh's mother answered: "Tell her she was his English mother. Thank you."

Minh's mother was very **superstitious**. She brought a red envelope for Minh with lucky money. She said nobody should cut their hair before the graduation. She checked the **lunar** calendar for the best day. "In Vietnam, we follow the **zodiac**," she explained to Anna. "Minh was born in the Year of the Dragon. That's why he's brave." Anna was fascinated by the **superstition**.

Tom raised his glass. "**Cheers**!" he said. "Congratulations to Minh!" In Vietnamese, they said "Một, hai, ba, dzô!" Everyone tried to say it. Nobody could.

The graduation ceremony was at the university's great hall the next day. Minh wore a black gown and a strange square hat. His parents sat in the front row. When his name was called, he walked across the stage. The professor shook his hand and congratulated him. His mother cried. His father stood up and clapped. Grandma Rose shouted, "That's our boy!"

There was a short **ritual** at the start: a **priest** said a prayer for the students. A Buddhist **monk** from the city's temple was also there, and a Catholic **nun** who taught at the university. "The university welcomes every faith and every belief," said the speaker. At the end, everyone stood for the national **anthem**. Minh's father stood very straight, though he didn't know the words.

After the ceremony, Minh's parents wanted to see something **spiritual**. Minh took them to the cathedral, where a guide told them about a **saint** buried there eight hundred years ago. "**Pilgrims** used to walk here from all over England to **worship**," she said. Minh's mother lit a candle. "For Grandma," she said. "For a **blessing**."

Then they visited a war **memorial** by the river. Minh's father read the names silently. "Every country has these," he said. "Every country loses its sons." In Vietnam, too, families **mourned** for years after the war. Minh's grandmother had worn black for a year after her husband died. "**Mourning** is love with nowhere to go," his father said quietly.

Grandma Rose, who was with them, told them the **legend** of the river: a story about a princess who turned into water to save her people. "Every place has its **myths**," she said. "This river has many." Minh's father told her about the Vietnamese legend of the dragon and the fairy, the parents of all Vietnamese people. "We're all children of a dragon," he said. Grandma Rose said she always knew Minh was special.

That night, Minh's parents asked him a difficult question. "Will you come home now?" his mother asked. "You have your degree. Your job in England is finished."

Minh did not know what to say. Grace had offered him a job at the Gazette. Hana was staying in England to work at the hospital. And the Parkers were his family too. But his real family was in Vietnam.

"I don't know, Mum," he said honestly. "I need time to think."

His father put a hand on his shoulder. "We didn't come here to take you home," he said. "We came to **commemorate** your success. Whatever you decide, we'll **bless** it. You're a man now. It's your life."

Later, Minh learned that his father had asked Tom many questions about British **citizenship**, visas and work. His father had already started to accept that his son might stay. It was the most **festive** week of Minh's life. It was also the most confusing.
