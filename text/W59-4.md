# The Will

In June, the family met with a lawyer to read Grandma Rose's will. It was short and practical, like her.

She left the house to Tom and Anna. She left her jewelry to Mia, including the ring from her husband and the bracelet Margaret had given her. She left her books to Leo. She left her recipe book, handwritten over seventy years, to Rose Mai. "To be kept by Minh until she's old enough to cook without burning the kitchen."

And she left one more thing: a **benefaction**, a gift of money, to the hospital, "for the nurses, who are angels, as Arthur Bell said." It was enough to pay for a new rest room for nurses and a training fund. The hospital named it "Rose's Room."

Hana cried when she heard. "She remembered Arthur," she said. "After all these years."

There were other stories that month, as life continued.

The podcast's **listenership** reached a new record. "How to Live" was still being shared. Minh got letters from people all over the world. A man in Brazil wrote that he had called his father after ten years. A woman in Korea wrote that she had forgiven her sister. "Your grandmother changed my life," wrote many.

Ffion gave a talk about **agrochemical** pollution and the river trust's success. "The river is cleaner than at any time in two hundred years," she said. "That's because of **doggedness**: years of hard work."

A local school used **memorisation** of poems as part of a new English program. Some people thought it was old-fashioned. Lan defended it on the podcast. "Memorisation has value," she said. "But not alone. Understanding first."

Lan also talked about grammar: **attributive** adjectives, which come before a noun ("a red car"). And she explained that some people are **unilingual**: they speak only one language. "Most of the world isn't," she said.

Helen Ward told the story of the town's **seigneurial** past: the old lords who owned everything. "We think we're far from that," she said. "But wealth is still unequal. Some families still own most of the land."

A report on the **breadline** showed that many families in Britain were near poverty. **Undernourishment** among children was rising. The Gazette ran a campaign for free school breakfasts. The council agreed.

Hana talked about health: **corpulence**, an old word for being very overweight, and why people sometimes **overindulge** in food when stressed. "Grief can do that," she said. "Be kind to yourself."

Leo's sanctuary welcomed a **bioengineered** prosthetic leg for a three-legged dog. "Science is amazing," said Leo. The dog ran in circles all day.

The university's science department explained DNA on the podcast for children: "**deoxyribonucleic** acid," said the scientist slowly. Rose Mai repeated it perfectly. Everyone was astonished.

The council used **computerisation** to speed up planning applications. Some people complained that the system lacked **representativeness**: it didn't listen to all voices. Minh's reporter investigated.

The Gazette faced a **detractor**: a national columnist who wrote that local newspapers were "dying dinosaurs." Minh replied in an editorial. "Our readership has tripled in ten years," he wrote. "That claim is **contestable**, at best." The columnist didn't reply.

Someone damaged the new memorial plaque for Grandma Rose in the park: **defacement** with spray paint. The town was angry. The next morning, ten volunteers cleaned it. By noon, it was perfect again. "That's Riverford," said Daniel.

Minh thought about his **forebears**: his grandparents in Vietnam, Grandma Rose here. Their **industriousness** had built everything he had. Their **unprejudiced** kindness to strangers had made him who he was.

Rose Mai was adjusting, slowly. She was learning to **acclimatise** to life without Grandma Rose. Her **acclimatisation** was helped by the recipe book. Every Sunday, she and Minh made one recipe from it. Her first was the cake. It was a little burnt. "Grandma would say it's perfect," said Rose Mai.

"Grandma would say it's burnt," said Minh, laughing. "And then eat three pieces."

He updated his **biodata** for the Gazette's website that month. Under "family," he wrote: "Wife Hana, daughter Rose Mai, and two families in two countries, one of which included the best grandmother in England." The board said it was too personal. He didn't change it.

Some costs would have to **retrench** at the Gazette that year. But Minh promised: no job cuts. They would save in other ways. Grandma Rose had taught him that people come first.
