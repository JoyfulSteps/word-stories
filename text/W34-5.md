# The Best Week and the Worst Week

In September, two things happened in the same week. One was the best thing in Minh's life. The other was one of the worst.

On Monday, a box arrived from London. Inside were ten copies of his book, *Two Rivers*. Real books, with his name on the cover. Minh opened the box with shaking hands. He held a copy. He smelled the pages. He was **ecstatic**.

Hana came home and found him sitting on the floor, surrounded by books. "Is that it?" she said. "Your book?"

"My book," said Minh. He felt **elated**, **exhilarated**, completely happy. "It's real."

They celebrated with a bottle of champagne and a lot of sushi. Minh sent photos to everyone. His parents were **astonished**. His father, who couldn't read English, held the book upside down in the photo. Lan was **content** just to see her name in the thank-you page. The Parkers were **contented** and proud. Mrs. Green at the library ordered twenty copies.

But on Thursday, a message came from England. It was from Mia.

"Minh. I'm sorry. Margaret died last night. In her sleep. Grandma is **devastated**."

Minh read it three times. He sat down. Hana came and held his hand. He couldn't speak.

Margaret, the funny, kind old woman who painted rivers. Margaret, who had found her best friend after sixty years. They had had only two years together. Two years after sixty years apart.

Minh called the Parkers. Anna answered. Her voice was **gloomy** and tired. "She's not good," Anna said. "Rose. She's not eating. She just sits by the window."

Minh asked to talk to Grandma Rose. There was a long pause. Then her voice came, small and old.

"Minh," she said. "Did you hear?"

"I heard. I'm so sorry, Grandma."

"I keep thinking," she said, "about the sixty years we wasted. All those years. Because of pride. Because of a silly argument." Her voice broke. "I should have written back in 1975. I was **insecure**, and proud, and stupid."

"You found her again," said Minh. "You had two good years. That's not nothing."

"It's not enough," said Grandma Rose. And then she cried.

Minh felt helpless, eleven thousand kilometers away. He felt **dismayed** and sad. He wanted to fly to England immediately, but the funeral was in Scotland in three days, and he couldn't get a flight in time.

That night, he couldn't sleep. He felt **bewildered**: how could the best week of his life also be one of the worst? He felt guilty for being happy about his book while Grandma Rose was suffering. He also felt **apprehensive**. Grandma Rose was eighty-four. How long did she have?

Hana understood. "**Grief** is strange," she said. "It comes in waves. You can't control it. People **grieve** in different ways. Some cry. Some get angry. Some become **indifferent** for a while, like nothing matters."

"How do you know so much about it?"

"I'm a nurse," said Hana. "I see grief every week."

The next day, Minh did something. He wrote a letter to Grandma Rose. A real letter, on paper, like the one Margaret had sent in 1975. He wrote about Margaret: her paintings, her jokes, her smile when she saw Grandma Rose on the garden path. He wrote: "You didn't waste sixty years. You found her. That's the end of the story that matters. Not the beginning."

He put a copy of his book in the envelope. On the first page, he wrote: "For Rose and Margaret. Two rivers that met again."

A week later, Grandma Rose called. Her voice was stronger. "I read your letter," she said. "And your book. All of it, in two days. My eyes hurt."

"Are you okay?" asked Minh.

"No," she said honestly. "But I'll be okay. Your book helped. And your letter. You're very **empathetic** for a young man. And very **courageous** to write about your own life like that. I'm not **humiliated** by the grandmother character, by the way. Although she's very bossy. I can't imagine who she's based on."

Minh laughed through his tears.

"Margaret would have loved the book," said Grandma Rose. "She told me once that you were **witty**, **humorous**, **diplomatic**, **dependable**, **courteous** and **frank**. She was **gracious** about everyone, but she really liked you. She said you were **versatile**: journalist, café worker, detective. That was her favourite **trait** in a person."

"I'm **exasperated** that I can't be there," said Minh.

"Don't be," said Grandma Rose. "Come at Christmas. That's soon enough. And bring the girl."

After the call, Minh sat by the window of their small Tokyo flat. Outside, the city was bright and busy. He thought about Margaret, about rivers, about time. Then he opened his laptop and started to write. Not a story for the Gazette. Something else. Something about two old women and a river. Maybe, one day, it would be his second book.
