# The Fumes

In April, residents near the industrial park started to complain. There was a strange smell in the evenings. Some people had headaches. Children were coughing. "It's the **fumes** from the factory," said one mother. "I'm sure of it."

The factory made plastic parts. Its owners said everything was safe. They showed reports: the air was clean, they said. But the complaints continued.

Then an **informant** contacted the Gazette: a worker at the factory. She was afraid of losing her job, so she wanted to stay anonymous. "The reports are **deceptive**," she said. "They test the air on Mondays, when the machines are off. On Thursdays and Fridays, when they work all night, nobody tests anything."

Minh **pondered** what to do. It was a serious claim. If true, the company was lying and people were being harmed. If false, the Gazette could be sued. He decided to investigate carefully.

Ellie and a new reporter, Sam's cousin Lucia, borrowed air-testing equipment from the university. They tested the air near the factory every day for a month. On Mondays, the air was fine. On Fridays, the levels of some chemicals were **grossly** above the legal limit.

"This is not a small problem," said Ffion, who checked the data. "Calling it a 'slight increase' would be an **understatement**. These chemicals can **inflict** real damage on lungs, especially children's."

They could now **pinpoint** the problem: the night shifts, when an old machine was used **improperly**. The company had saved money by not fixing it.

Before publishing, Minh contacted the company. The director was angry at first. Then the evidence was too clear. A week after the story, the government's environment agency investigated. The company was fined: a **hefty** sum, half a million pounds. It also agreed to a payout to families affected, and to fix the machine.

The story was **divisive** in the town. Some workers were angry at the Gazette. "You could have closed the factory," said one man. "Two hundred jobs." Others were grateful. "My son has asthma," said a mother. "Now I know why it got worse."

Minh wrote an editorial. "Jobs and health are not enemies," he wrote. "A factory can be safe and successful. The problem was not the factory. It was the choice to save money at the cost of people's health."

There were other stories that month.

A report on **socioeconomic** differences in Riverford showed that families in the poorest area had more health problems and **deficient** diets. "The link between income and health is clear," said the report. The council promised more **subsidized** housing and free school meals. "We need a more **equitable** town," said the mayor.

Helen Ward talked about the history of German **unification** in 1990 on the podcast, **analogous**, she said, to small towns joining together. "Unity is hard," she said. "But it can **surpass** expectations."

A **benevolent** businessman gave money to restore the old **ornamental** garden by the castle. "It was beautiful when I was a child," he said. "I want my grandchildren to see it."

Leo told a story about the sanctuary: a dog found in a **bog**, a wet, muddy area outside town, trapped for two days. It survived. "Dogs are tough," said Leo. "Tougher than people."

Minh used a predictive model from the university to forecast how air quality would change after the factory fixed its machine. "It should improve within a month," said the researcher. "That's my **conjecture**, but the data supports it."

Grandma Rose listened to the episode. Afterwards, she called Minh. "You're brave," she said. "But be careful. People with money don't like being told the truth."

"I know, Grandma."

"Good." Then, more softly: "Your grandfather would be proud. He always said: **sincerity** is worth more than gold."

The month ended with a small **setback**: the company's lawyers sent a letter accusing the Gazette of mistakes. Minh's lawyer reviewed everything. There were no mistakes. The letter was a **jab**, not a real threat. The company didn't **invoke** any legal action. "They wanted to scare you," said Grace. "And soften the next story."

"It didn't work," said Minh.

"Of course not," said Grace. "You have the **prowess** of a real editor now. And the **worldly** wisdom to know when to ignore a letter."
