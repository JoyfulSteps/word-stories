# Buy, Buy, Buy

In March, Yuko asked Minh to write about shopping. "Tokyo is a shopping city," she said. "But is that good? Write about **consumerism**."

Minh started in the biggest shopping area in Tokyo. On a Saturday, millions of people were there. Every shop was full. People carried bags and bags of things: clothes, shoes, electronics, cosmetics. Many of them looked happy. Some looked tired.

He interviewed shoppers. A young woman showed him her bags. "I didn't plan to buy anything," she said. "It was an **impulse**. I saw a sale and I couldn't stop." She laughed. "I **splurged**. Three hundred dollars on clothes I don't need."

An older man was more careful. "I'm **frugal**," he said. "I buy only what I need. My parents grew up after the war, when there was nothing. They taught me to be **thrifty**." He showed Minh a small notebook where he wrote every yen he spent.

Minh talked to a shop manager. She explained how prices work. "We buy the **merchandise** from the factory, then we add a **markup**: usually two or three times the price. Then, when we want to sell old stock, we do a **markdown**. A **clearance** sale at the end of the season. Fifty, sixty, seventy percent off."

"So the normal price is too high?" asked Minh.

The manager smiled. "Some people would say **exorbitant**. But we have rent, staff, everything. And people pay."

Minh also visited a market where people sold **counterfeit** goods: fake designer bags, fake watches, fake sneakers. "Ten percent of the real price," said the seller, a young man. "Nobody can tell the difference." The police raided the market every few months, but it always came back.

He talked to an economics professor about it. "Japan was very **materialistic** in the 1980s," she said. "People bought everything. Expensive cars, expensive watches, an **extravagant** life. Then the economy crashed. There was a long **recession**. Many people became careful with money. Now, the young generation is different. Many prefer experiences to things."

She explained more. "A country's economy is like a family's. If you spend more than you earn, you have a **deficit**. If you earn more than you spend, you have a **surplus**. Japan's government has a big deficit. But Japanese families save a lot."

At the same time, Minh had his own money news. The Blue Cup in Riverford had its yearly shareholders' meeting. Minh had bought one share, as a **shareholder**, and he joined by video. Daniel explained the numbers.

"This year, our revenue was up twenty percent," he said. "After paying all the bills, the loan, the staff, we made a small profit. So for the first time, we can pay a **dividend** to our shareholders. Two pounds per share."

Everyone cheered. Two pounds was nothing. But it was something.

Daniel showed the accounts. "Our **assets**: the building, the equipment, the money in the bank. Our **liabilities**: the bank loan, which we'll pay for fifteen more years. Our **equity**: the difference, which belongs to all of you."

"Is it a good **asset**?" asked an old shareholder. "Better than a government **bond**?"

"No," said Daniel honestly. "A bond pays more. But a bond doesn't make you coffee."

Everyone laughed. Grandma Rose, who had twenty shares, said she would use her forty pounds to buy cake for everyone.

There was one more thing. Daniel had ordered new coffee from Vietnam, from the farmers Minh had found. But the shipment was stuck in **quarantine** at the port. "Customs is checking everything," he said. "Plants, food, coffee. Two weeks. Then it's ours."

He also had a subscription idea: customers could pay a monthly fee for a coffee every day. "Like a phone subscription. And if you sign up for a year, you get a **rebate**: one month free."

Minh wrote his article. He called it "Things." He wrote about the woman with her bags, the frugal man with his notebook, the counterfeit market, and the small café in England that paid its shareholders two pounds. "Maybe the question isn't how much we buy," he wrote. "Maybe it's what we buy, and why. A **commodity** is just a thing. A café is a place. A place is worth more."

Yuko read it. "It's a little philosophical," she said.

"Is that bad?"

"No," she said. "It's Saturday. People need philosophy on Saturday."
