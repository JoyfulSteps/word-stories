# Riverford Talks, Episode 16: Writing Workshop (Part 3)

Minh: Welcome back to our writing workshop. Sarah Lane is with me again. Today: problem-solution essays.

Sarah: Hello, everyone.

Minh: What's a problem-solution essay?

Sarah: The question describes a problem and asks for causes and solutions. For example: "Traffic in big cities is getting worse. What are the causes, and what can be done?" Students must explain the problem and suggest well-planned solutions.

Minh: Let's do that one. Causes?

Sarah: More people, more cars, poor public transport. In the developing-world, cities grow very fast, and roads can't keep up. In rich countries, people often prefer cars because public transport is slow.

Minh: Solutions?

Sarah: Many. Car-sharing schemes, where people share one car. Bike-share systems. Vehicle-free zones in city centres. Large-scale investment in trains and buses. Transport-led planning, where you build homes near stations. Mixed-use areas, with homes, shops and offices together, so people walk instead of drive.

Minh: Can you give examples?

Sarah: Copenhagen is famous for cycling. Paris created many foot-friendly streets. In some cities, a cross-border train connects two countries, so workers don't need cars.

Minh: Good. Now let's look at a student essay. A student from our Sunday club, a talented young man, wrote this introduction: "Traffic is a heat-related and stress-related problem in many cities. It affects rich-poor groups differently, because low-income people cannot afford cars and must use slow buses."

Sarah: That's a strong start. He shows the problem has different effects on different people. That's mature thinking. I'd simplify "heat-related" though: maybe "pollution-related" would be better there.

Minh: He'll be happy to hear that. He's a self-improvement fan. He listens to every episode twice.

Sarah: That's the right spirit.

Minh: What about compound adjectives in problem-solution essays?

Sarah: Very useful. "A carbon-free transport system." "A plastic-free city." "Chemical-free farming." "A device-free day." "A screen-related health problem." "A nature-related solution." They're concise and clear.

Minh: Some students worry they sound unnatural.

Sarah: Only if you use too many. Remember: one or two per paragraph. And make sure each one has a clear meaning. Near-native writers use them naturally. Learners should use them carefully.

Minh: What makes a solution convincing?

Sarah: Evidence and detail. Don't just say "the government should build more trains." Say how, and give an example. A teacher-led or student-focused approach in education essays, or even student-to-teacher feedback; a community-based approach in city essays. Show that you understand the problem in a non-technical way, without too much jargon.

Minh: Let's finish with a sample conclusion.

Sarah: "In conclusion, traffic is a complex problem with many causes. However, well-planned, multi-stage solutions, such as better public transport and car-free zones, can make cities healthier and fairer for everyone."

Minh: Perfect. Sarah, thank you.

After the episode, Minh got an interesting message from a listener, a famous-figure in the world of town planning: an award-winning architect who had designed record-breaking green buildings in Asia. He offered to come on the podcast. "Your listeners are asking the right questions," he wrote.

Minh was excited. "An **award-winning** architect on our little podcast," he told Grace.

"Don't be humble," said Grace. "Your podcast isn't little anymore."

The Sunday club also grew in a new direction. Some students wanted to study at graduate-level in English-speaking countries. Others needed advanced-degree English for research. Some were from single-parent families, or one-parent homes where they had to work and study. Two-parent families were not the norm in the club. Minh realised how hard many of them worked.

He started a small program for multi-language support. Students who spoke the same language helped each other. Self-testing quizzes were added, made by volunteers. One student, a non-academic but very clever man who drove a taxi, became the best at grammar. "Talent-based, not degree-based," said Minh. "That's how it should be."

There were also problems. Some students struggled with pre-existing anxiety. Some stopped coming because of non-payment of their internet bills: they couldn't afford the connection. Minh asked The Blue Cup's shareholders for help, and they paid for ten students' internet for a year.

"Consumer-created content," said Sam, who helped with the website. "That's what this is. The club creates its own lessons now. It's beautiful."

A multi-national group of listeners, from twenty countries, also started a time-ordered list of every essay topic in the podcast, with notes. Health-aware students added topics about food. Brother-sister pairs studied together. Self-confidence grew. Self-expression too.
