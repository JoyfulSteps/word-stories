# Riverford Talks, Episode 18: Writing Workshop (Part 5)

Minh: Welcome to the last part of our writing workshop. Sarah Lane is here for the final time this season. Today: health and the environment. Two of the most common exam topics.

Sarah: My favourites.

Minh: Let's start with health. A typical question: "Many people today live an unhealthy lifestyle. What are the reasons, and what can be done?"

Sarah: Reasons: time-pressured lives, where people don't have time to cook. Pre-packaged, nutrient-poor food that's cheap and easy. Stress-induced eating. Long hours at desks. Children spend too much time on screens instead of outdoors.

Minh: Solutions?

Sarah: Education: teaching children to cook. Better food in schools: vitamin-packed, locally-sourced meals. Some schools serve iron-enriched or nutrient-enhanced food to children who need it. Pesticide-free, allergen-free options. Wellness-oriented workplaces, with gyms and healthy canteens. And a focus on well-being, not just weight.

Minh: In my experience, Vietnamese food is healthy, but fast food is growing there too.

Sarah: It's a global trend. Good to use your own country as an example.

Minh: Now the environment. A typical question: "Cities should be made greener. Discuss."

Sarah: Ideas: tree-planting programs. Pedestrian-friendly streets. Zero-emission public transport, or at least low-emission buses. Anti-congestion charges, where drivers pay to enter the city centre. Bicycle-sharing schemes. Sustainability-focused planning. Eco-development and eco-travel. Emission-free zones. Some cities limit ride-hailing cars in the centre. Young people are more eco-aware than ever.

Minh: That's a long list.

Sarah: Choose two or three and explain them well. Depth is better than length.

Minh: What about people-centred design?

Sarah: Very important. Cities should be people-centred, not car-centred. The same applies to schools: child-centred, student-centred, learner-centred education. The idea is that the system serves people, not the other way around.

Minh: Let's talk about technology in essays. Many students write about it.

Sarah: Yes. Algorithm-driven social media, ML-driven recommendations, computer-aided design. These are modern topics. Students should know the vocabulary but not overuse it. And they should give two-sided arguments: technology has benefits and dangers.

Minh: One more topic: families. Many questions ask about changes in family life.

Sarah: Good vocabulary: multi-generational homes, cross-generational relationships, lone-parent families, child-minding by grandparents. Post-pandemic changes: more people working from home, more time with family, but also more stress.

Minh: And bullying? We did an episode about that.

Sarah: Anti-bullying and anti-cyberbullying programs are common essay topics. Students should mention school policies, parents' roles and online platforms.

Minh: Let's finish with your best advice for the whole series.

Sarah: Read widely. Write regularly. Get feedback. Be patient: improvement is time-consuming. And remember: an essay is a conversation with the reader. Be clear, be honest, be kind-hearted with yourself when you make mistakes.

Minh: Sarah, thank you for five wonderful episodes.

Sarah: Thank you, Minh. It's been a pleasure.

The writing workshop series was the podcast's most successful ever. Schools used it. Teachers shared it. The Sunday club, which had started with ten Vietnamese students, now had two thousand members from fifty countries. It was crowd-sourced: volunteers ran groups in different time zones. Minh couldn't run it alone anymore. He didn't need to.

In the Gazette, Grace wrote an article about it. "A small podcast from a small town has become a well-structured, global classroom," she wrote. "It began with one immigrant who remembered what it was like to struggle."

Minh read the article twice. Then he called his parents. His father answered. "Your mother is at the market," he said. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong," said Minh. "I just wanted to tell you something. The club I started, the free English club, has two thousand students."

His father was quiet. Then he said, "When you were a boy, you helped me fix motorbikes. You always wanted to fix things. Now you fix people's English." He paused. "Your grandmother would say: that's a good use of a life."

"Is Grandma okay?"

"She's ninety. She's strong. She asks about you every day."

After the call, Minh sat by the window of the flat above the bakery. He thought about all the people in the club: the climate-displaced student from Bangladesh who now studied engineering; the law-breaker who had been in prison and was rebuilding his life through English; the single mother in Manila who wanted to be a nurse. Each one had a story.

He thought about his own story too. A multi-storey building of a life, he thought: each floor built on the one below. Riverford, Tokyo, Hanoi. The Parkers, Hana, Lan. The café, the newspaper, the podcast, the club.

There was one more floor to build, he thought. He didn't know what it was yet. But he knew it would be hand-crafted, like everything good. Not pre-packaged, not algorithm-driven. Built by hand, with people. Even the law-breakers, he thought, can change, if someone gives them a chance and a well-structured path.

Hana came home from the hospital. She had worked a long shift. She was tired. But she had news.

"Sit down," she said. "I have something to tell you."

Minh's heart jumped. "What is it?"

Hana smiled. "We're going to need a multi-generational home," she said. "Or at least a bigger flat. In about seven months."

Minh couldn't speak. Then he laughed, and cried, and hugged her for a very long time. Outside, the river ran on, as it always did, toward the sea.
