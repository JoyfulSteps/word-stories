# The Appeal

In October, Minh got a surprising phone call. It was the mother of the young man from the burglary trial, Andrei. "My son is making an **appeal**," she said. "He has new evidence. Please come. Nobody else will listen."

Minh talked to Grace. "We reported the trial," she said. "If there's new evidence, we should report that too. Go. But be careful. Don't take sides."

Minh visited Andrei in prison. It was a strange, cold place. Andrei looked thinner and older. "I admit I made mistakes," he said. "I took things from two houses. I **deserve** my punishment for that. But the other three burglaries, it wasn't me. People **blame** me because it's easy. I'm the foreigner."

"What's the new evidence?" asked Minh.

"A camera," said Andrei. "A neighbor's door camera. On the night of one of the burglaries, it shows the real burglar. It's not me. It's someone taller, older. My lawyer found it last month. The police never checked."

Minh was not easily **convinced**. "Why didn't your lawyer find it before?"

"He didn't look. He thought I was guilty. Everyone did."

Minh went to see the lawyer, a new one, a young woman called Ms. Clarke. She showed him the video. It was not very clear. But the man in it was **barely** visible in the dark: tall, in a black coat. Andrei was short.

"Is this enough to win the appeal?" asked Minh.

"**Provided** the judge accepts it, yes," said Ms. Clarke. "**Supposing** the judge thinks the video isn't clear enough, then no. It's a challenge. But we have to attempt it."

Minh wrote a careful story. He didn't claim that Andrei was innocent. He didn't **criticize** the police. He just explained the new evidence. Considering the facts, he let the readers think for themselves.

Some readers were angry. "Why are you defending a criminal?" one wrote. "He **boasts** about being innocent, but he admitted to two burglaries!" Another wrote: "Thank you for expressing the other side. Everybody **deserves** a fair trial."

The appeal was in December. Minh went. Andrei's mother sat in the front row, holding a small cross. The judge watched the video three times. She asked many questions. The police officer who had led the investigation had to confirm that they never checked the door camera. "An honest mistake," he said. "We were very busy."

The judge thought for a long time. Then she made her decision. Andrei was guilty of two burglaries, excluding the other three. His punishment was reduced to one year. He had **scarcely** two months left to serve.

Outside the court, Andrei's mother hugged Minh. She was crying. "Thank you," she said. "You listened."

"I didn't do anything," said Minh. "The lawyer did."

"You wrote the story. After that, people started to look at us differently. Apart from my son's lawyer, nobody else cared."

Andrei came out of prison in February. He came to The Blue Cup to see Minh. He was quiet and nervous. "I want to start again," he said. "Find work. Be normal. But it's hard. When people see 'prison' on your CV, they disappear."

Daniel was listening. He was quiet for a moment. "I needed someone to help in the kitchen," he said. "Washing dishes, mostly. It's not exciting. But it's a job. **Providing** you work hard, you can stay."

Andrei couldn't speak. Then he said, "Why? Why would you trust me?"

"Because somebody once was willing to **forgive** me for a stupid mistake," said Daniel. "A long time ago. Everybody needs one person to encourage them. Following that, it's up to you."

Andrei started work the next Monday. He was quiet, hard-working and always on time. After a month, Pat said he was the best dishwasher she had ever had. After three months, Daniel let him handle the cooking on Sundays.

Minh wrote about it a year later, including both men's stories, in a story called "Second Chances." He combined Andrei's story with Pete's. Both had fallen. Both were getting up. "**Aside** from luck," he wrote, "the thing that generates change is people. One person who sees you. One person who doesn't look away."

Grace put it on the front page. The story attracted more letters than anything the Gazette had published that year. Even the angry reader wrote again. "I was wrong," he wrote. "**Lest** we forget: we're all human." Later, Grace talked to Minh about it. "Some readers approved of your story and some didn't," she said. "You won't gain everyone's approval. Don't try. A good story can expand, extend into people's lives and change them. Nobody can deny that. But never attach yourself to one side, and never let your feelings **explode** on the page. Our job is to determine what exists, what the story really contains, what it consists of. Sometimes we challenge what everyone thinks. Sometimes we bend a little, to listen. It was Andrei versus the state, but really it was about people."

"Is that hard for you?" Minh asked. "To stay neutral?"

"For **oneself**, it's always hard," said Grace. "That's why we do it together."
