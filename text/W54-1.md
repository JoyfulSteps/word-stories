# The Gift

In September, the Gazette received an unexpected letter. It was from a lawyer in London. A woman who had grown up in Riverford, and had died at ninety-eight, had left money in her will: an **endowment** of two million pounds for "independent journalism in my home town."

Minh read the letter three times. Then he called Grace.

"Two million?" said Grace. "Who was she?"

Her name was Dorothy Webb. Minh and Ellie researched her life. She had left Riverford at eighteen, studied at Oxford, and become one of the first female science journalists in Britain. She had written about space, medicine and the environment for sixty years. She had never married. She had **devoted** her life to her work.

"She wrote about **celestial** objects, stars and comets, when nobody thought women could understand science," said Helen Ward, who knew of her. "She was a pioneer."

The endowment was a **watershed** moment for the Gazette. For the first time in its history, it would have secure money for the future. "This changes everything," said Minh at a staff meeting. "We can **flourish** now, not just survive."

But Minh was careful. He knew money could change things. He set up a system: the money would be managed **systematically** by an independent board. It would **uphold** the paper's independence. Nobody, not even Minh, could use it for anything except journalism.

The town's reaction was **overwhelmingly** positive. People were proud that a woman from Riverford had done this. The council decided to **erect** a small statue of Dorothy in the library garden. It would be the first statue of a woman in the town.

Minh wrote a long article about her life. He found her old articles in an archive. In one from 1960, she wrote about the dangers of **methane** gas from farms and landfills. "Decades before anyone talked about climate change," said Ffion, amazed. "She saw it coming."

In another article, from 1975, she wrote about the **medicinal** uses of plants in traditional cultures. "She was always looking at things others **overlooked**," said Helen.

There were other stories that month.

The town's economy began to **rebound** after a difficult year. New businesses opened. Unemployment fell. "The **backbone** of the town is small business," said Daniel. "When small business does well, everyone does well."

Leo reported on the river. After years of pollution, the water was cleaner. Wildlife was returning. "Conditions are **favourable** for fish now," he said. "The chemical **residue** in the mud is lower. We're on the **brink** of something good." He planned a special project for the next spring.

Helen Ward told the story of a dark time in Riverford's history: the 1600s, during the English Civil War. "There was **turmoil** everywhere. Families divided. Some people fought against what they called the king's **tyranny**. Others fought for him. In the **ensuing** years, Riverford was occupied three times." She talked about how quickly things could change. "**Swiftly**, a peaceful town can become a battlefield. **Radically**, everything can change in a year."

Minh's own life was changing too. He was invited to speak at a national conference on local journalism. He had to send his CV, his **vita**, to the organisers. Hana helped him write it. "Ten years ago, your CV was one page," she said. "Now look."

At the conference in London, he met many journalists. Some he had known for years; others he was only **acquainted** with. He gave a talk about the Gazette, the podcast and Dorothy's endowment. People were interested. "Local news is dying everywhere," said one editor. "You've found a way to make it live."

"Not me," said Minh. "The town. And a woman who loved it."

He came home tired but happy. Rose Mai was asleep. Hana was reading. Minh sat by the window and looked at the river.

"What are you thinking?" asked Hana.

"About Dorothy," said Minh. "She left everything to a town she left at eighteen. Seventy years later. She never forgot it."

"Will you forget Vietnam?" asked Hana.

"Never," said Minh. "But I think I understand Dorothy now. You can love two places. One where you start. One where you live. And your **outward** life can be in one place while your heart is in both."

Dorothy's gift was **invaluable**, Minh thought. Not the money. The example. The **prominence** of a forgotten woman, finally recognised. He decided the Gazette would give an annual prize in her name, for young science journalists. **Lax** standards would never be accepted. Only the best.
