# The Intensive Care Unit

In June, Minh wrote his first article for the Gazette from Japan. Grace wanted something personal. "Write about Hana's work," she suggested. "People in Riverford remember her. And our hospital has the same problems."

Hana got permission for Minh to visit her hospital, a huge modern building in the center of Tokyo. She worked in intensive care, with the sickest patients.

A senior **physician**, Dr. Tanaka, showed Minh around. "Here, we treat people after major operations," he said. "Heart surgery. Organ **transplants**. Accidents. Some patients come with a **syndrome** we don't fully understand, or a rare **disorder** of the blood."

He showed Minh a patient, an old man who had received a new kidney the week before. "His **recovery** is good," said Dr. Tanaka. "But we must watch for **inflammation** and infection. The body sometimes fights a new organ."

Another patient was a young man who had taken an **overdose** of medicine by mistake. "Too big a **dose**," said Dr. Tanaka. "Very dangerous. We saved him, but only just."

Minh watched Hana work. She moved quickly and calmly. She gave a patient medicine through a tube. She checked machines. She talked softly to a woman who was waking up after an operation. "The **anesthetic** makes them confused," she explained. "They don't know where they are. I tell them: you're safe."

Later, Minh met other members of the team. A **pharmacist** who checked every medicine. A **paramedic** who brought patients in by ambulance. A physiotherapist who helped with **rehabilitation**, teaching people to walk again after long illness.

"The hardest part," said Hana, "is when patients have a **relapse**. They get better, they go home, then they come back sicker. It breaks your heart."

Minh asked Dr. Tanaka about health problems in Japan. "We live long," the doctor said. "But we have new problems. **Obesity** is rising, especially in young people. Fast food, no exercise. In poorer countries, the problem is the opposite: **malnutrition**. Not enough food. And everywhere, **hygiene** and **sanitation** are important. Clean water and clean hands save more lives than doctors."

"What's the best **remedy** for staying healthy?" asked Minh.

Dr. Tanaka laughed. "Sleep, vegetables, walking, and friends. **Wellness** is not only the body. Most **ailments** start with stress."

Minh thought about his own flu.

He also noticed how different the patients looked. In the waiting room, there was a **plump**, cheerful grandmother with a **chubby** baby. A **stocky** construction worker with a broken hand. A **petite** woman, very small, who turned out to be a famous dancer. She was **graceful** even in a hospital gown. Next to her, a tall young man was very **clumsy**; he knocked over a cup of water twice.

"People think hospital patients are **unattractive**," said Hana. "Sick, gray, tired. But look. Everyone here has a story. Everyone is beautiful in some way."

One patient was especially **striking**: an old woman with white hair and bright eyes, who was ninety-five and recovering from heart surgery. She asked Minh where he was from. When he said Vietnam, she smiled. "I visited Saigon in 1960," she said in English. "Beautiful city. Beautiful people."

Minh wrote his article that night. He called it "The Quiet Heroes." He compared Hana's hospital in Tokyo with the hospital in Riverford. Different countries, different languages. The same problems: too few nurses, too many patients, and people who worked too hard because they cared.

Grace published it on the front page. Hundreds of readers commented. One of them was Kate, Grandma Rose's niece, the nurse in Riverford. "Thank you," she wrote. "For seeing us." There was also a comment from Arthur, the old man Hana had cared for. "My angel has gone to Japan," he wrote. "Lucky Japan."

Hana read the comments and cried. "I miss Riverford," she said.

"Me too," said Minh. "But we'll go back."

"Yes," said Hana. "We'll go back. But first, we work."
