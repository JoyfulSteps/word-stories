# The Food Market

On the day after Christmas, there was a winter food market in the nearest town. Minh wanted to write about it for the Gazette's food page. Hana and Mia came with him.

The market was in an old stone square, full of stalls with lights and music. It smelled of hot wine, roasting nuts and fresh bread.

The first stall sold grains. There were big bags of **oats**, for porridge, and **barley**, for soup. There was **quinoa**, a small seed from South America. "Very fashionable," said the woman at the stall. "Young people love it. Full of protein." There was **wholegrain** rice and **wholemeal** flour for bread. "Better for you than white," she said. Mia bought a bag of oats for Grandma Rose.

The second stall sold meat. There was Scottish **venison**, deer meat, dark and strong. There was **veal**, from young cows. "Very tender," said the butcher. Hana didn't buy anything. "I'm not brave enough for venison," she said.

The fish stall was Minh's favourite. Scotland was famous for seafood. There were black **mussels** in a big bucket, white **scallops** in their shells, and small **clams**. There was **squid**, which Minh knew well from Vietnam. There were small silver fish: **anchovies** and **sardines**. There was **mackerel**, with blue and black stripes, and big white **cod**, the fish used for fish and chips.

"In Vietnam, we'd grill the squid with chili and lime," said Minh to the fisherman.

"In Scotland, we fry everything," said the fisherman, laughing. "But you can grill it if you want. Just don't tell my wife."

The vegetable stall had winter vegetables. There were **artichokes**, which looked like green flowers, and dark green **kale**. There were **turnips**, white and purple, and **parsnips**, like white carrots. "Parsnips are sweet when you roast them," said the farmer. "Essential for Christmas dinner." There were small **shallots**, like little onions, and long green **scallions**. "In America, they call those green onions," said Mia. "My dad would know."

The last stall sold spices and herbs. It was run by an old Indian man who had lived in Scotland for fifty years. He had everything: red **saffron**, the most expensive spice in the world, "more expensive than gold." There was **nutmeg**, brown and round, for Christmas cakes. There were green herbs: **oregano** for pizza, **thyme** and **rosemary** for meat. And there was fresh **coriander**.

"In America, they call this **cilantro**," said Mia.

"Your dad again?" asked Hana.

"Always my dad."

The Indian man laughed. "Coriander, cilantro, it doesn't matter. In Hindi, we call it dhania. Same plant, many names. Like people." He gave Minh a small bag for free. "For your Vietnamese soup," he said. "Coriander is good in everything."

Minh interviewed all the stall owners. He took photos. He tried everything. By the end, he was very full.

On the way back to the cottage, Mia asked, "What will you write?"

Minh thought. "That food is like a map," he said. "Every food came from somewhere. Quinoa from South America. Saffron from Iran. Coriander from everywhere. Venison from these hills. And all of it, in one small square in Scotland, at Christmas."

"That's nice," said Hana.

"It's a bit sentimental," said Mia. "Grace will say so."

"Grace always says so," said Minh. "Then she publishes it anyway."

That night, they cooked the squid, Vietnamese style, with chili and lime and the free coriander. Grandma Rose tried it. "Spicy," she said, her eyes watering. "Margaret would have hated it." Then she took a second piece. "But I don't."
