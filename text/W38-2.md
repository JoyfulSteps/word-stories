# The Fraud

In April, a big scandal hit Tokyo. A famous company that sold investment products had collapsed. Thousands of people had lost their money. The company was **insolvent**: it had no money left to pay anyone. The police said the managers had committed **fraud**.

Yuko gave the story to Minh and Min-ji, the persistent Korean reporter. "Follow the money," she said.

The story was complicated. The company had promised **lucrative** investments: twenty percent profit every year. "Too good to be true," said an expert. "And it was."

The managers had **embezzled** money from their customers. They used it for luxury cars, houses and holidays. They also moved money through companies in other countries to hide it. "That's money **laundering**," said a police officer. "Making dirty money look clean."

Minh and Min-ji interviewed victims. It was hard. An old couple had lost their savings. They had been **affluent** once; they owned a nice house. Now they were **impoverished**. "We trusted them," said the woman. "They were so friendly. So professional." Another man was completely **destitute**. He had lost his house and lived in a small room. "I borrowed money to invest," he said. "I used my house as **collateral**. Now the bank has it."

The victims were all **creditors** now: the company owed them money. But there was nothing to pay them with. "The **debtor** has no money," said a lawyer. "The creditors will get almost nothing back."

The scandal had big effects. The government talked about new rules. There was a debate about **fiscal** policy, government spending and taxes, and **monetary** policy, interest rates and money. Some politicians wanted a **subsidy** for the victims. Others said the government couldn't **subsidize** bad investments. One politician wanted a new **levy** on investment companies. Another talked about **tariffs** on foreign goods, which had nothing to do with the story.

"Politicians always talk about what they want to talk about," said Min-ji.

After two weeks, Minh was tired. The story made him sad. Japan was **prosperous**, one of the richest countries in the world. But **prosperity** didn't protect everyone. Even in a rich country, people could lose everything.

That weekend, he needed something happy. He called his sister Lan in Vietnam. She was eighteen now, in her last year of high school. She was bright, ambitious and very busy.

"How's school?" he asked.

"Exhausting," she said. "Exams, exams, exams. In Vietnam, every subject is **compulsory**. Maths, literature, English, history, science, everything. In England, Mia says students can choose. They have **optional** subjects. **Electives**. Here, nothing is optional. Everything is **mandatory**."

"What about **extracurricular** activities?" asked Minh. "Clubs, sports?"

"I'm in the English club and the debate club. And I teach poor kids English on Sundays. My teacher says I'm good at it. She says my **pedagogical** skills are natural. That I could be a teacher."

"Do you want to be a teacher?"

Lan was quiet for a moment. "I want to study **pedagogy**. The science of teaching. Abroad. Like you."

Minh was surprised. And very proud. "Where?"

"Japan," said Lan. "Or England. I'm applying for scholarships. Will you help me?"

"Of course," said Minh. "Of course I will."

After the call, he felt better. The world had fraud and greed. But it also had seventeen-year-olds teaching poor kids English on Sundays. He had an **overdraft** on his bank account that month, from too many trips, but he didn't care. That night, he started to research scholarships for Lan.
