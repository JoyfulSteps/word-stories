# Tokyo Kitchen

Back in Tokyo, Yuko gave Minh a new job at the newspaper: a weekly food column. "You wrote about food in England," she said. "Now write about food in Japan. For foreigners who live here."

Minh loved it. Every week, he went somewhere new: a tiny noodle shop, a fish market at four in the morning, a fancy restaurant where the meal cost more than his rent.

His first column was about an izakaya, a Japanese pub, near the train station. He went with Ben, the Australian, and Aiko, the quiet writer from Osaka. They were **starving** after work. "I'm **famished**," said Ben. "I could eat a horse."

The food was **appetizing** and cheap. There was grilled chicken on sticks, **succulent** and juicy. There was beef that was so **tender** it fell apart. There were small plates of vegetables, tofu and fish. Ben ate everything. "This is **gluttony**," he said happily. "And I don't care."

They also drank. Ben drank beer. Aiko drank **sake**, Japanese rice wine, served warm in a small cup. Minh tried it. It was strong and sweet. "Careful," said Aiko. "Sake is stronger than it tastes."

Minh was careful. Ben was not. He tried everything on the drinks menu: Japanese whisky, **gin**, **rum** with cola, a sweet plum **liqueur** and, at the end, a glass of French **brandy**. By midnight, he was very **intoxicated**. He sang loudly on the street. The next morning, he came to work with a terrible **hangover**. "Never again," he said, holding his head. "I'm **dehydrated**. I need water. And coffee."

"Too much **caffeine** won't help," said Aiko. "Drink water. And maybe **kombucha**. It's good for the stomach."

Ben looked at her like she was crazy. "What's kombucha?"

"Fermented tea. A little sour. Very healthy."

"I'll stick with coffee."

Minh was not a big drinker. He usually stayed **sober**. Hana didn't drink at all; she was **teetotal**. "In the hospital, I see too many people hurt by alcohol," she said. "I'd rather have tea."

For his second column, Minh went to a cooking class. The teacher was a famous chef who taught Japanese and Western cooking. She showed them many techniques.

"To **saute**, you cook small pieces quickly in a hot pan with a little oil," she said, moving the vegetables fast. "To **sear** meat, you cook the outside very hot, so it's brown and crispy, but the inside stays pink."

She showed them how to **braise** beef: cook it slowly in a little liquid, for hours, until it's soft. How to **poach** an egg: cook it gently in hot water, not boiling. How to **caramelize** onions: cook them slowly until the sugar turns them sweet and brown. How to **knead** dough for bread: push and fold it with your hands for ten minutes.

At the end, she showed them how to **garnish** a plate: a little green herb on top, a line of sauce, a few seeds. "People eat with their eyes first," she said. "If it looks beautiful, it tastes better."

Some of the students' food was **palatable**. Some was **unpalatable**. Minh's braised beef was good, but his poached egg was a disaster. "It looks like a cloud," said the chef. "A sad cloud."

The chef also talked about **vegetarianism** in Japan. "Japanese Buddhist monks have eaten vegetarian food for a thousand years," she said. "Tofu, vegetables, mushrooms. Many people think Japanese food is all fish. It's not."

She gave them a final tip: "A little **cumin** in a beef stew. Not Japanese, but delicious." Minh wrote it down.

That week, he wrote his column: "Tokyo Kitchen: Learning to Cook, Learning to Fail." Readers loved it. One of them wrote: "Thank you for the honest description of your egg. Mine look the same."

Yuko read it and almost smiled. "Good," she said. "Next week: the oldest sake brewery in Japan."
