# Mia in Tokyo

In May, Mia came to Tokyo. She had an exhibition at a small gallery. A Japanese curator had seen her work online and invited her.

Mia was twenty now, in her second year at art school in London. Her style had changed. She still painted, but she also made **installations**: big works that filled a whole room. For Tokyo, she made a room full of hanging paper lanterns, each one painted with a different face from Riverford.

Minh and Hana helped her set it up. It took three days. "Art is hard work," said Mia, standing on a ladder. "People think we just sit and wait for inspiration."

The gallery also showed other kinds of art. There was a **mural**, a huge painting on a wall, by a young Japanese artist. There was a **mosaic** made of tiny pieces of colored glass. There were **ceramics**: bowls and cups made by a ninety-year-old potter. And there was a photo of an old **fresco** from an Italian church, a painting made on wet plaster six hundred years ago.

"Every art form has its own **aesthetics**," said the curator. "Its own rules of beauty."

On the opening night, many people came. A Japanese film director was there. He liked Mia's work so much that he asked her to help with his next film. "I'm making an **adaptation** of a famous novel," he said. "I need someone to design the visual world. The **cinematography** is very important. Every scene must be **cinematic**, like a painting."

He told them about the story. "The **protagonist** is a young woman who travels to a foreign country. The **antagonist** is her own fear. There's no villain. The **narrative** is very quiet."

"That sounds like Minh's book," said Mia.

The director had read Minh's book in Japanese translation. "It was **acclaimed** here," he said. "**Critically**, it was very successful. Your **prose** is simple but beautiful."

Minh was surprised. He didn't know his book had been translated into Japanese.

"Your publisher didn't tell you?" said the director. "It came out last month."

The next day, Minh found his book in a big bookshop in Shinjuku. It was on a table with other new books. The cover was different: a painting of two rivers meeting. In the **foreword**, a famous Japanese writer had written an introduction. Minh couldn't read it, so Hana translated.

"'This book is about belonging,'" Hana read. "'The author writes like a poet. His sentences are like **verse**. He uses **metaphors** and **similes** sparingly but well. There is **irony**, but also great warmth.'"

Minh felt strange. People he had never met were reading his words.

That week, he also got an email from Diana, his agent. "A publisher wants to include one of your short stories in an **anthology**," she wrote. "A collection of stories by young writers from Asia. And they want to know: is your second book finished? Send me the **manuscript**."

His second book was almost finished. It was about Grandma Rose and Margaret. It was not a **memoir**, a true story, but it was close. He had changed the names and some events. Some critics would call it a kind of **allegory**: a story with a deeper meaning about friendship and forgiveness.

"What about poetry?" asked Mia. "Have you ever written poems?"

"Once," said Minh. "In England, at the library. It was terrible. Every **stanza** was too long. I couldn't make anything **rhyme**."

"Poetry doesn't have to rhyme," said Mia.

"Mine didn't have to be terrible either," said Minh. "But it was."

Mia laughed. Then she said, "Write a **satire** next. About the conglomerate that bought your newspaper."

"That's a good idea," said Minh. He wrote it down.

Mia's exhibition was a success. Every lantern was sold. The film director hired her for his film. She was going to spend the summer in Tokyo.

"Two Parkers in Japan," said Hana. "And two Nguyens. Riverford is moving east."
