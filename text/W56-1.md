# The Tycoon

In September, a famous **tycoon** came to Riverford. His name was Victor Lang. He had made billions in property and retail. He owned shopping centres, hotels and a chain of cafés. And he wanted to buy half of Riverford's high street.

His plan was simple: a **buyout** of twenty small shops, turned into one big shopping centre. "It will bring jobs and investment," he said at a press conference. "Riverford is **uncharted** territory for big business. I see opportunity."

The town was divided. Some shop owners wanted to sell; they were tired and the money was good. Others refused. Daniel was one of them. "The Blue Cup is not for sale," he said. "Not for any price."

Minh's team investigated Lang's business history. They found a pattern. In other towns, Lang had bought high streets, then closed small shops and replaced them with his own chains. "**Merchandising** is his real business," said an expert. "Every town ends up looking the same."

They also found evidence of **dishonesty**. In one town, Lang had promised to keep local shops. Two years later, he closed them all. "The promises were **unchecked**," said a former councillor from that town. "Nobody made him keep them."

The Gazette published the story. It was careful and fair: it included Lang's response and the views of shop owners who wanted to sell. But the facts were clear. The story **amplified** the debate across the region.

Lang was angry. He called the Gazette's owner. He offered to buy the newspaper too. The owner laughed. "The Gazette is protected by Dorothy Webb's endowment," he said. "It can't be sold."

The town council held a public meeting. Hundreds came. After a long debate, the council refused Lang's planning application. "We want a high street with character," said the mayor. "Not a copy of every other town."

Lang left. "Small towns are **touchy**," he said to a national newspaper. "They don't like change."

Minh replied in an editorial. "We like change," he wrote. "We don't like being bought."

There were other stories that month.

The river trust started a project to restore the old **waterway**: a canal from the 1800s that connected Riverford to the next town. Volunteers cleared rubbish and plants. "It's land **reclamation**," said Leo. "Taking back what was lost."

The council announced help for **carers**: people who look after sick or old family members, often without pay. "They save the health system billions," said Hana. "They deserve support."

The new youth council debated **antisocial** behaviour: some teenagers had been damaging the park at night. Instead of more police, the youth council proposed a new evening sports program. It worked. Problems fell by half.

A family of refugees arrived in Riverford as part of a national **resettlement** program. Minh interviewed them. They had escaped war. "We need **fortitude**," said the mother. "Every day is a new start."

Lan visited from Tokyo and talked on the podcast about exam culture in Asia. "Students **cram** for exams: studying all night, memorizing everything," she said. "It's not real learning. **Attainment** is high, but stress is too."

A university professor talked about artificial intelligence. "Some people talk about a 'technological **singularity**': a moment when AI becomes smarter than humans. I'm not sure it will happen. But we must think about it."

Helen Ward talked about a historical **insurrection** in 1830, when farm workers rose against new machines that took their jobs. "History repeats," she said. "Then machines, now AI."

The council faced a **budgetary** crisis: less money from the government. "We must **minimise** waste," said the finance officer. "**Attrition** of staff, not cuts. When people retire, we don't replace them all." It was not popular.

Minh's third book was nearly finished. He called it his **opus**, his biggest work: the history of Riverford through its people. His publisher was excited. "**Captivating**," the editor said after reading the first chapters. "The **stylistic** mix of history and journalism is unusual."

Minh's **workflow** had changed: he wrote from five to seven every morning, before Rose Mai woke up. Then the Gazette. Then home. "It's **observational** writing," he told Hana. "I just watch the town and write what I see."

"You've watched it for ten years," said Hana.

"And I'm still surprised every day," said Minh.

Some **geopolitical** news touched the town too: a trade dispute between big countries raised the price of coffee. Daniel had to **procure** beans from new suppliers. "Even the price of a cappuccino in Riverford depends on world politics," he said. Then **deregulation** of the energy market raised electricity bills. Everything was connected.
