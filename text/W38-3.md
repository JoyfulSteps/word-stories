# Lan's Application

For two months, Minh helped Lan with her scholarship applications. It reminded him of his own application, five years ago. Ms. Huong had helped him then. Now he was helping his sister.

Lan wanted to study education at a university in Tokyo. The university had a special program for international students. It was a famous university, **accredited** in many countries. Its **accreditation** meant that its degrees were respected everywhere.

The application was long. Lan needed to show her **proficiency** in English with a test score. She was already very **proficient**; her English was better than Minh's had been at her age. She also needed to take an **aptitude** test, a test of her natural ability.

"Why do they need all this?" asked Lan. "My grades are good."

"They want to see how you think," said Minh. "Not just what you know. Japanese universities like **analytical** students. Students who can solve problems."

Lan was worried about one thing. "In Vietnam, we learn by **rote**," she said. "We memorize everything. The teacher talks, we write, we memorize, we repeat. I'm good at that. But am I good at thinking?"

"You're very good at thinking," said Minh. "You just haven't had the chance to show it."

Minh asked his friend Dr. Thu, the Vietnamese scientist, for advice. She was a **scholar** now, with many **scholarly** articles. She had also been on the university's selection committee.

"The **dean** of the education faculty is very interested in students who want to improve their own countries," she said. "Tell her about your Sunday classes. That's more important than grades."

Lan wrote an essay about her Sunday students. One of them was a boy who couldn't read well. The teachers at his school said he was lazy. Lan noticed that he mixed up letters: b and d, p and q. She researched it online. "I think he has **dyslexia**," she wrote. "A difference in how the brain reads. It's not about intelligence. **Dyslexic** children are often very bright. They just need a different way to learn."

She found a **remedial** reading program online, free, and used it with him. After six months, he could read. "He's actually **gifted** at maths," she wrote. "Nobody knew, because he couldn't read the questions."

"That's the essay," said Minh, when he read it. "That's it."

Lan sent everything in May. Then she waited. Minh knew that feeling well.

While they waited, Minh learned more about Japanese **tertiary** education: universities and colleges after high school. The head of a university, the **chancellor**, was usually a famous professor. **Alumni**, former students, gave money and helped new students. One famous **alumnus** of Lan's chosen university was a Nobel Prize winner.

"What happens if I get in?" asked Lan. "Can I **matriculate** in September?"

"If you get the scholarship, yes. You'd start in September."

In June, the email came. Lan opened it during a video call with Minh, his parents and their grandmother all watching.

She read it. She read it again. Then she screamed.

"Full scholarship!" she shouted. "Tuition, housing, everything! And a small **remuneration** for teaching assistant work. They pay me to help teach!"

Their mother cried. Their father stood up and walked around the room. Their grandmother said, "Two children abroad. Who will take care of me?" But she was smiling.

Minh felt so proud he couldn't speak. His little sister, the one who listened at the door during his interview. The one who wrote "Hello, England" on his T-shirt.

"You'll be in Tokyo," he said finally. "In the same city as me."

"I know!" said Lan. "You'll have to take care of me!"

"Hana will take care of you," said Minh. "I'll just buy you dinner."

That night, he thought about **vocation**: the feeling that you are meant to do something. Lan had found hers: teaching. It was not only a **livelihood**, a way to earn money. It was a calling. The **compensation** was small. The **incentive** was not money. It was the boy who learned to read.

He wrote to Ms. Huong, his old English teacher in Vietnam. "My sister is following the path you showed me," he wrote. "Thank you. You changed two lives." Ms. Huong replied the next day: "That's the best **appraisal** a teacher can get." He thought she was an **intellectual** in the best sense: someone who used her mind to help others.
