# The Birthday Dinner

On the last day of December, it was Tom's fiftieth birthday. Anna made a **reservation** at the oldest restaurant in Riverford, The Old Bell. It was more than a **century** old. "It has been here for over a hundred years," Anna said. "Grandma Rose had her wedding dinner there."

The family arrived at seven o'clock. The restaurant was beautiful, with candles, old pictures and white tablecloths. A **waitress** came to the door. "Good evening. Do you have a **booking**?"

"Yes," Anna said. "Parker, six people, seven o'clock."

The waitress **checked** her book. She looked again. Her face changed. "I'm very sorry," she said. "I have Parker for seven o'clock... next Friday."

Everybody was quiet for a moment. Anna's face went red. "Oh no. I'm so sorry, Tom."

But Tom laughed. "It's fine! Let's try somewhere else."

It was New Year's Eve, and every restaurant was full. They walked and walked. Leo said, "We can get a **takeaway**, or order a delivery pizza." Mia said, "Or we can eat at the college **canteen**." "It's closed," said Minh. "And the **cafeteria** food is terrible."

Suddenly, Tom stopped in front of a small, bright place with red seats and a long counter. It was an American **diner**. "Look!" he said. "Burgers, milkshakes, pancakes! It's like home!"

They went in immediately. It was warm and noisy. The waitress gave them the menu. There was no buffet and no fancy food, but there was everything Tom loved. "Of **course**, I want the biggest burger," he said.

They **shared** plates of fries and onion rings. The waitress **served** a huge chocolate cake with fifty candles. Everybody sang. The food had a wonderful **smell** of grilled meat and sweet sauce. It was not fine **dining**, but it was a **feast**.

"Fifty years," said Grandma Rose, looking at her son. "Five **decades**. I remember the day you were born. It was a long period of my life, and the best."

"What about the future, Dad?" Mia asked. "What do you want for the next fifty?"

Tom thought. "A **barbecue** every summer, a weekly picnic by the river on Sundays, and this family. That's all. And maybe, once or twice a year, a better restaurant booking."

Everybody laughed, even Anna.

At the end, Tom paid the bill and asked for the **receipt**. He left a big **tip** on the table. "In America, we always tip," he said. "Fifteen or twenty percent." "In England, ten percent is fine," said Grandma Rose. "In Vietnam, we usually don't tip at all," Minh added.

"**Lately**, I've been thinking," Tom said on the walk home. "This is a daily life I love. Not the big things. These small moments."

At midnight, the town counted down and fireworks went up over the castle. Minh thought about his family in Vietnam. **Recently**, he called them every week, but tonight he called them at midday their time... no, he did the maths: it was already morning there. They were sleeping.

So the next morning, Minh got up at **dawn**. He walked to the bridge alone to watch the **sunrise** over the river. The sky turned pink and gold. He sent a photo to his family. His mother replied immediately: "Happy New Year! Your father says you look thin. Eat more."

Minh smiled. Some things never change. Like the monthly money worries, the yearly birthday cake and Mum's annual message about eating more. Later that day, he walked by the river again at **dusk** and watched the sunset. He wanted to remember this day forever.
