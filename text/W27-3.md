# The Digital Newspaper

The Riverford Gazette had a problem: fewer people bought the paper version every year. "Young people don't buy newspapers," said Grace. "They read everything on their phones. We need a better website. We need to be on social media. We need a new strategy."

She gave Minh a new task. "You're young. You understand this. Help us."

Minh was not an expert, but Sam was. Sam's design firm was small, but they had made websites for many companies. Minh asked him for help. Sam came to the office with his laptop. He looked at the Gazette's homepage and made a face.

"This looks like 2005," he said. "The links don't work. You can't scroll properly on a phone. There's no touchscreen design. And look at this icon. Is that a newspaper or a sandwich?"

Grace laughed. "That's why we need you. Our objective is simple: more readers. The paper has no shares, no rich owners, just us."

Sam made a **proposal**. He wrote it like a professional. There was an **agenda** for the first meeting. He made a presentation with a spreadsheet of costs. At the meeting, they discussed it. Minh wrote the minutes. Then they had to negotiate the price. The Gazette had very little money.

"Look," said Sam. "I'll do it cheap. In return, you put my company's logo on the website. Free advertising."

"Deal," said Grace.

They started a campaign to launch the new website. They made a slogan: "Riverford Gazette: Your Town, Your News." Sam designed it. Ellie became a blogger and wrote a blog about life in Riverford. Bill the photographer started a podcast where he told stories about his forty years of photos. Minh started writing a weekly column about The Blue Cup and the people who came there.

The new website was very efficient. It worked on phones, laptops and tablets. It had a simple login page. You could make a profile with a username, change your settings, and subscribe to the newsletter. Readers could leave a comment under every article. The efficiency of the system surprised Grace. "In the old days, it took a week to change anything," she said. "Now it takes a second."

But there were problems too. One night, a hacker tried to hack the website. Sam had to update the security. Someone put spam in the comments, hundreds of messages selling medicine. The inbox was full of strange emails with dangerous attachments. "Never open an attachment from someone you don't know," said Sam. Grace said she understood. Then she opened one. Sam had to uninstall three programs from her computer.

Minh learned a lot about technology. He learned about coding: Sam showed him how the code behind the website functioned. He learned how a database stored every article. He learned how to use different browsers to test the website. He learned that every sensor in a phone, the GPS that tells you where you are, the wireless connection, the Bluetooth for your headphones, all sent information. "Your phone knows everything about you," said Sam. "Every tab you open. Every time you zoom in on a photo. Where your cursor goes."

"That's frightening," said Minh.

"That's modern life," said Sam.

The new website was a success. In the first month, the number of readers doubled. Young people started to follow the Gazette online. Ellie's blog had a thousand followers. Bill's podcast was streaming on every app. The paper version still sold to old people, and the digital version reached everybody else.

The Gazette was no longer just a **competitor** to the big newspapers. It was a local partnership: the paper, the café, Sam's company, the town. A big agency from London called Grace. They wanted to buy advertisements. Their **agent** said: "Your local content is very valuable. People trust local news."

Grace was delighted. At the next **conference** of local newspapers, she gave a talk about their success. She wrote a memo to the staff afterwards: "Thank you, everybody. Especially our intern, who brought his clever friend. Our policy from now on: digital first."

Minh read the memo three times. Then he forwarded it to his parents in Vietnam. His father, who didn't read English, asked Lan to translate. Then he called Minh. "Your boss says thank you to you?" he said. "In writing? Then you are doing well, son."

"I'm just an intern, Dad."

"An intern who gets a thank you letter," said his father. "That's not just an intern."

Minh smiled. He put his phone down. On his desk, there was a new voicemail from Grace: "Minh. Come early tomorrow. Big story. You and Ellie. Bring your notebook." He wondered what it could be. He didn't have to wait long to find out. He would soon learn to operate in a real newsroom on a real case. The USB stick in his pocket held all his notes. The operating system of his old laptop was slow, but he didn't care. He was ready.
