# Riverford Talks, Episode 2: Work

Minh: Welcome to Riverford Talks. This week's question: why do we work? And how is work changing? My guests are Daniel Hughes, owner of The Blue Cup café, and Dr. Priya Shah, an economist at the university. Welcome to both of you.

Daniel: Thanks for having us.

Priya: Happy to be here.

Minh: Daniel, you left a job in a bank to open a café. Why?

Daniel: Shame, honestly. Not shame about the bank. Shame about myself. I was making good money, but I hated every day. I felt it was a **shame** to waste my life. My dad's words inspired me: "Do what you love, or love what you do." So I quit.

Minh: And do you love it?

Daniel: Most days. Not the days when the coffee machine breaks. But I'm **inspired** by the people. Every morning, the same faces. A café is a small community.

Minh: Priya, is Daniel typical? Are more people leaving big jobs for small ones?

Priya: It's not typical, but it's growing. After the pandemic, many people thought about their lives. They asked: what is the importance of my work? Is it worth the stress? **Typically**, people only change jobs for money. Now, many change for meaning.

Minh: What else is changing?

Priya: Technology, mostly. Robots and computers are doing more jobs. In manufacturing, for example, factories need fewer workers than before. In **mining**, machines do the dangerous work. Even in offices, software does many tasks. Some jobs will disappear. New ones will appear.

Minh: Should people be worried?

Priya: Somewhat. There will be some **resistance** to change, as there always is. In history, every new technology met resistance. But in the end, more jobs were created than lost. The question is whether people can learn new skills fast enough.

Daniel: Can a robot make coffee?

Priya: It can. But can it remember your name and ask about your daughter? That's the difference.

Daniel: Good. I'm safe then.

Minh: Let's talk about different ways of working. Daniel, your café is owned by its customers. A **joint** ownership. How does that work?

Daniel: Five hundred people own shares. We have a meeting once a year. They vote on big decisions. It's a combination of a business and a club. Some people thought it would be a **massive** problem: too many owners, too many opinions. But it works. Mostly.

Priya: That's actually a growing model. Co-operatives, joint ownership, employee-owned businesses. Unlike traditional companies, the profits stay in the community.

Minh: What about people who work alone? Freelancers, solo workers?

Priya: Their number is growing fast. The good side is freedom. The bad side is risk. No fixed salary, no sick pay. Depending on the job, it can be wonderful or terrible.

Minh: Daniel, what's the best thing about running a small business?

Daniel: Freedom. And the **serving**. I mean, the moment of serving someone something good. A good **serving** of cake, a warm cup. It sounds random, but it makes me happy.

Minh: And the worst?

Daniel: Paperwork. Taxes. Requirements from the council. Every year, more rules. And the warning letters, if you make a small error.

Priya: That's every small business owner's favourite complaint.

Minh: Last question to both. What's your advice to young people starting work?

Priya: Learn to learn. The skills you need will change. The ability to learn new ones is the most valuable thing.

Daniel: Find work that matters to you. And be kind to the people you work with. Good relations at work make everything easier. Info and money are important. But people are more important.

Minh: Daniel and Priya, thank you.

After the episode, The Blue Cup got many new customers. People came in and said, "I heard you on the podcast." Daniel put up a sign: "As heard on Riverford Talks." Minh said it was the first time anyone had used his name in an advertisement. "Get used to it," said Daniel.

The café's special **offering** that week was a "podcast cake": chocolate and coffee combined in one. It sold out by noon. Nearby shops asked to sponsor the next episode. Even the mayor, who was elected on a promise to support local business, came in for a slice. Daniel's bell over the door rang all day.

That night, after closing, Daniel sat with Minh. "People want to talk about real things," he said. "Not just celebrities and football. That's the hole you're filling." He laughed. "Max one episode a week, though. My kitchen can't take more cake."

Two weeks later, the university introduced a public session on the future of work. It was intended for students, but it was open to all citizens. People from the arts, from shops and from the old factories came. The flow of ideas was amazing. One old factory worker talked about the origin of the town's industry: for a hundred years, Riverford made goods for the whole country, until the factory closed. "It was a blow to the town," he said. "But we survived." Minh thought the evening was an object lesson in what a town could do when people talked. At the ending, everyone stood up, and the motion of three hundred chairs sounded like thunder.
