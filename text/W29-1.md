# Try Everything

In February, Grace had an idea for a new series in the Gazette. "Minh, you're going to try a different hobby every week for two months," she said. "And write about it. We'll call it 'Minh Tries Everything.'"

"Everything?" said Minh.

"Everything," said Grace. "Readers will love watching you fail."

Week one was sport. Minh went to the sports center. The first class was **archery**. He had a bow and arrows. The target was twenty meters away. His first arrow went into the grass. His second arrow went into the wall. "Good **stamina**," said the teacher kindly. "Bad aim."

Then he tried **fencing**, fighting with thin swords. He wore a white suit and a mask with metal **equipment**. His opponent was a twelve-year-old girl. She beat him in thirty seconds. Then **wrestling**: he lasted ten seconds. Then **weightlifting**: he could not lift the bar. "You need more strength," said the coach. "And less Vietnamese coffee."

Week two was water sports. He tried **rowing** on the river with the university club. Eight people in one long boat, all pulling together. It was hard but beautiful. Then **canoeing**, alone, which was easier until he fell in. He did not try **scuba** diving because the river was too cold. "Next summer," he promised.

Week three was extreme sports. Leo taught him to ride a **skateboard** in the park. He fell eleven times. A little boy laughed at him. Mia filmed everything for the website. "This is gold," she said. Sam told him about a **snowboard** holiday in France, and a friend who did a **triathlon**: swimming, cycling and running, one after another. "No," said Minh. "Never." And **mountaineering**? Climbing real mountains with ropes? "Absolutely never."

At the end of every sports class, Minh learned to **stretch** his muscles, so he would not hurt himself. He still hurt everywhere.

Week four, he watched a professional cricket match as a **spectator**, to understand the game. An old **umpire** in a white coat stood in the middle and made decisions. Every few minutes, he blew his **whistle**... no, wait. Cricket umpires don't use a whistle. They use their fingers. Minh learned this the hard way when he wrote it in his first draft and an angry reader corrected him. At the end, the winning team lifted a silver **trophy**. Minh understood nothing about the game, but he understood the sportsmanship: both teams shook hands and had tea together.

Week five was **crafts**. He went to a **pottery** class and tried to make a bowl. It looked like a hat. He tried **origami**, Japanese paper folding. Hana taught him to make a paper bird. His bird looked like a fish. "It's a fish-bird," said Hana generously.

Week six was quiet **handicraft** at Grandma Rose's house. She taught him **embroidery**, sewing pictures on cloth, and **crochet**, making things from wool with a small hook. "In my day, every girl learned these," she said. "Now, nobody." Minh made a small crochet square. Grandma Rose put it on her wall. "Your first **craft**," she said. "I'm proud."

Week seven was games. He did a **jigsaw** puzzle with a thousand pieces, which took three days. He did the **crossword** in the newspaper with the old judge from The Blue Cup. He learned **sudoku**, the number puzzle. It was too easy for him. "Maths again," he said.

Week eight was **nightlife**. Mia and Sam took him to **karaoke**. He sang a Vietnamese song, very badly. Everyone clapped anyway. Then Sam wanted to go to a **nightclub**. The music was so loud that Minh couldn't hear his own thoughts. He went home at midnight. "I'm too old for this," he said. He was twenty-two.

His articles were very popular. People stopped him in the street. "You're the man who fell off the skateboard!" they said. Grace was delighted. "Your best series yet," she said.

"What's the lesson?" she asked, for the final article.

Minh thought. "Every **pastime** looks easy until you try it," he wrote. "But trying is the fun part. You don't need to be good. You just need to start."
