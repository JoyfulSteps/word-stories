# The Night Shift

Hana worked night shifts at the hospital once a week. They were long and hard. One night in June, she came to Minh's flat at seven in the morning, still in her uniform. She sat on the sofa and didn't say anything.

"Bad night?" asked Minh.

"The worst," she said.

He made her tea and sat next to her. Slowly, she told him.

At two in the morning, an ambulance brought in an old man. He was vulnerable: eighty-nine, living alone, with no family nearby. He had fallen at home and lay on the floor for a day before a neighbor found him. His condition was severe. He was cold, confused and very weak.

"The doctor said it was urgent," said Hana. "We had to work fast. His heart was unlikely to survive the night."

Hana stayed with him. She did everything specific to his care: checked his heart, gave him fluids, kept him warm. But mostly, she held his hand. He couldn't speak much. His hand was cold and **stiff**. But at one point, he opened his eyes and looked at her. "Are you an angel?" he asked.

"No," said Hana. "I'm a nurse."

"Same thing," he said, and closed his eyes.

His name was Arthur. He had been a teacher for forty years. In his wallet, there was a photo of his wife, who had died ten years ago, and a **precious** letter from a former student, thanking him for changing her life.

"He was so alone," said Hana. "Nobody came. Nobody called. A whole life, and at the end, only me."

"But you were there," said Minh. "That matters."

Hana shook her head. "It's not sufficient. Hospitals are sophisticated now. We have **superb** machines, **superior** medicine. But we don't have time. Vital things, like sitting with someone, holding their hand, we can't always do. There are too few nurses. The overall problem is substantial. It's a universal problem, in every country."

Arthur did not die that night. In the morning, he was still alive. Weak, but alive. The doctor was surprised. "Remarkable," he said. "His heart is stronger than we thought."

"Maybe he didn't want to leave while someone was holding his hand," said Hana.

Minh thought about it for a long time. Then he had an idea. "Can I write about him?" he asked. "Not his name. Not private things. Just the story. Old people alone. Nurses with no time."

Hana was **reluctant** at first. Patient privacy was important. But Arthur, when he was stronger, said yes. "Write it," he said. "Make people think. Maybe it'll help the next one."

Minh wrote the story carefully. He wanted it to be **precise**, **thorough** and relevant. He talked to doctors, nurses and social workers. He found the visual data: graphs showing how many old people lived alone in Riverford. The numbers were vast: one in three people over eighty. He found a charity that organized voluntary visitors for lonely old people. They needed help.

The story was **straightforward**, but it had a **profound** effect. It was the most **prominent** story on the Gazette's website for two weeks. People called the charity to volunteer. A hundred people signed up in a month. Several of them were young students. "I never thought about it before," one said. "It's such a **subtle** problem. You don't see it. Old people are silent. They don't complain."

Arthur got a visitor every day after that. A young woman who studied history visited him on Mondays. They talked about the past. A retired man visited on Wednesdays. They played chess. A particular family visited every Sunday with their children. Arthur, who had no family, suddenly had many.

When he finally went home, the volunteers organized a secure system so someone checked on him every day. Hana visited him too, on her days off.

"You made a **tremendous** difference," she told Minh.

"No," said Minh. "You did. I just wrote it down."

"We both did," said Hana. "That's the ultimate team."

Arthur sent them both a card. Inside, in shaky handwriting, he wrote: "To my angel and my reporter. Thank you for seeing me. Your unique friend, Arthur."

It was valid proof, Minh thought, that small things still matter. The potential for kindness is everywhere, even in a busy, suspicious world. Arthur's memories of his prior life as a teacher were still **vivid**, and now he had a productive new life too, full of chess and history. "I feel privileged," he told Hana. "At eighty-nine, I have more friends than I had at thirty." Hana became very protective of him. On the upper floor of the hospital, where the oldest patients slept, the nurses put up a sign with the charity's number, visible to every visitor. It was a **rational**, simple idea. Arthur said it made him feel like a **tough** old man again.
