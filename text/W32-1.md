# A Hundred Years

In May, the Riverford Gazette was a hundred years old. For the special edition, Grace asked Minh to write a profile of the newspaper's history. "And no, you can't write about me," she said.

Minh wrote about her anyway. He just didn't tell her.

He started with the **historical** facts. The paper was started in 1925 by a schoolteacher who wanted the town to have "a decent, honest newspaper." For the entire century, it had come out every week, even during the war, when paper was short and half the staff were soldiers. "It was never rich," said Bill, the old photographer. "But it was always **faithful** to the town."

Then he wrote about Grace. He interviewed everyone who worked with her. They all said the same things, in different words.

"She's tough," said Raj. "**Fierce**, sometimes. But fair. She's the most capable editor I've ever worked with. She can do everything: write, edit, design, sell advertisements. And she's consistent. She treats a councillor and a cleaner exactly the same."

"She's **genuine**," said Ellie. "What you see is what you get. There's no hidden Grace. If she's angry, you know. If she's happy, you know. Well, you rarely know if she's happy. But you know."

Minh also talked to people outside the newspaper. The mayor said Grace was "a constant critical voice," which was not entirely a compliment. A businessman said she was "**hostile** to local business." A teacher said she was "essential to democracy in this town." Opinions were multiple and complex. But one thing was evident: everyone had an opinion about Grace.

Then he talked to Grace's father, his landlord, Mr. Okafor. Mr. Okafor told him the story Grace never told anyone.

Grace came to England from Nigeria when she was eight. Her family had very little money. At school, other children were cruel. "They called her names," said Mr. Okafor. "**Intense**, every day. But she never cried at school. She cried at home, and then she studied. She read every newspaper in the library. She said: one day, I'll write the news, and I'll write it fairly. For everyone."

She became a journalist at twenty-two. It was challenging: she was a young Black woman in a world of old white men. Some editors were **hostile**. Some were **liberal** and kind. She worked for distant big newspapers in London for twenty years. Then, at forty-five, she came back to Riverford to run the little Gazette. "Everyone thought she was crazy," said Mr. Okafor. "Leaving London for this? But she said: small towns need good journalism most."

Two years ago, the owners wanted to close the paper. Grace fought. It was an exhausted, confusing time. She convinced a local businessman to buy it. She cut her own salary in half. "The **mere** idea of Riverford without a newspaper made her sick," said her father. "She has strong **moral** ideas. Right is right. Wrong is wrong."

Minh asked Mr. Okafor one more question. "Is she happy?"

Mr. Okafor thought. "She's fortunate," he said. "She does what she loves. That's rare. Happiness is a different question. Maybe you should ask her."

Minh wrote the profile. It was detailed and honest. He showed the good and the complicated sides of Grace. He was not **neutral**, exactly, but he tried to be fair. He wrote that some people found her difficult. He also wrote about the eight-year-old girl who cried at home and studied.

He gave it to Raj, not Grace. Raj read it and was silent for a long time. "Put it in," he said. "I'll take the responsibility."

When the special edition came out, Grace read the profile in her office. She closed the door. She didn't come out for an hour.

When she came out, her eyes were red. She walked to Minh's desk. Everyone in the newsroom held their breath.

"That was **inevitable**, I suppose," she said. "Somebody was going to write it one day. I'm glad it was you." She paused. "It was **outstanding**. Thank you."

Then she went back into her office and closed the door again.

"I think she liked it," Ellie whispered.

"I think she did," said Minh. He felt an incredible, immediate joy, almost **identical** to the day his scholarship letter came. Most stories become invisible the day after they're published. This one did not. People talked about it for weeks. Raj said it was effective because it was logical and honest, not excessive or sentimental.

After that, the respect between Minh and Grace was mutual. They still had occasional arguments. But both were conscious of the dense history behind every page, and both knew that the existing problem, how to keep the paper alive, was crucial. It was not convenient for either of them, but it was definite: they were a team. Even the internal politics of the office became more civil. The initial shock passed, and whatever their individual feelings, nobody thought the profile had been **harmful**. Grace even framed it, though she hung it in the toilet, "where it can't make me proud in public."
