# The Third Wedding

In April, they had their third wedding, in Scotland, at Margaret's cottage by the river.

It was small: only thirty people. The Parkers, of course. Grace from the Gazette. Daniel and Pat from The Blue Cup. Sam and his new girlfriend. Fiona, Margaret's daughter. Gareth, Megan and Ffion from Wales. Lan and Minh's parents flew from Vietnam; Hana's parents flew from Japan. It was the first time both families had been in England.

Grandma Rose had made Hana's dress, as she had promised. It was white silk, simple and beautiful. She was eighty-seven now and her eyes were weak, so Mia had helped with the small stitches. But the design, the cutting and the sewing were all Grandma Rose's.

"It's my legacy," she said. "My last dress. The best one."

The ceremony was in the garden, by the river. The spring flowers were out: the bulbs that Mia and Fiona had planted three years ago, red and yellow and white. A local registrar performed the ceremony. It was short and simple.

Then there were speeches.

Tom spoke first, as the "English father." He was funny and emotional at the same time. "When Minh came to our house, I tried to teach him American English," he said. "He learned British English instead, to annoy me. That's when I knew he'd be family."

Mia spoke next. She showed a painting she had made: two rivers meeting, with faces from every chapter of Minh's life. "There's no **stereotype** for a family," she said. "No single framework. You build it yourself."

Then Grace spoke. She was not good at speeches, she said, but she would try. "I've worked with many young journalists," she said. "Most have talent. Few have **integrity**. Minh has both. His **stance** on truth has never changed, even under **scrutiny**, even when it was hard. I'm proud to have been his editor. I'm prouder to be his friend."

Then Grandma Rose stood up. Everyone went quiet. She held Leo's arm for balance.

"I want to talk about rivers," she said. "This river ran past my friend Margaret's house for forty years. She painted it every day. She wrote me letters by it, letters she never sent. She was waiting for me."

Her voice shook. She stopped, then continued.

"We lost each other for sixty years because of a **misconception**: we thought pride was more important than love. That's the **paradox** of youth: you think you have all the time in the world, so you waste it. Minh and Mia found her for me. They gave me two years with my best friend before she died. That was the greatest gift of my life."

She looked at Minh and Hana.

"The **essence**, the **premise** of a good marriage is simple. Talk. Forgive. Never let an argument last longer than a day. And never, ever let pride win. That's my **rationale**. That's all I know."

Nobody had dry eyes. Even Grace was wiping hers.

After the speeches, there was a **dialogue** between the two families in three languages. Lan translated between Vietnamese and English. Hana translated between Japanese and English. Sometimes they made mistakes, and everyone laughed. The **implication** of all this, Minh thought, was clear: love was the only language everyone understood.

Minh and Hana walked down to the river alone for a moment. The water was fast and clear in the spring sunshine.

"Three weddings," said Hana. "Three countries. Three rivers."

"The **transition** from one life to the next," said Minh. "That's what this feels like. A new chapter."

"What's next?" asked Hana.

Minh had been thinking about it for months. He had a scenario in his head, a plan. But he didn't want to **speculate**. Not yet.

"Let's talk tomorrow," he said. "Today, let's just be here."

They stood by the river for a long time. Behind them, the party continued: music, laughter, Leo telling everyone about orangutans, Mia painting the guests, Grandma Rose sitting in Margaret's chair with a glass of whisky, smiling.

There was a mechanism to happiness, Minh thought: people, place, time. And a **momentum**: once it started, it kept going. He didn't want to analyze it. He wanted to live it.

There was no **speculation** about whether the day had been perfect. It had been. Everyone could **infer** that from Grandma Rose's face. The **inference** was easy. And her **ideology**, if she had one, was simple: love first, pride never. It would be the ethics of their marriage too. They had both learned that **vulnerability** was not weakness. Grandma Rose's **inclination** was to keep everything short, but this day she let it run long. There was a **provision** of cake for everyone, as there always is in a Grandma Rose **paradigm**. No **prerequisite** for joy except showing up. And the **validity** of the day, Grace said, didn't need checking. It was **persuasive** on its own.
