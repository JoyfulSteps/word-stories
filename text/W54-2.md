# Neighbours

In October, the podcast covered a strange story. Two neighbours in Riverford had been fighting for three years. It started with a tree.

Mr. Grant had a big oak tree in his garden. Mrs. Patel, next door, said its branches blocked her light and its roots damaged her wall. She asked him to cut it. He refused. The argument grew. There were letters, lawyers, shouting in the street. Both families were exhausted. The exhaustion was obvious on their faces.

Finally, the council suggested **mediation**: a meeting with a trained mediator who helps people find a solution. Both agreed, reluctantly. And both agreed to be on the podcast afterwards.

The mediator, a calm woman called Ruth Okoye, explained the process. "I don't decide who's right," she said. "I help people listen to each other. Often, the real problem isn't the tree. It's something deeper."

She was right. In mediation, it came out that Mr. Grant's late wife had planted the tree forty years ago. "Cutting it felt like losing her again," he said on the podcast. Mrs. Patel was surprised. "I never knew," she said. "If he'd told me, I would never have asked."

They found a solution together. A professional would cut the branches carefully, keeping the tree healthy. They would share the cost. Mrs. Patel offered to help Mr. Grant with his garden; he was eighty and struggling. By the end, they were friends.

"It's **noteworthy**," said Ruth, "how often anger hides sadness."

The episode was popular. People wrote with their own neighbour stories. Minh realised how many small conflicts there were in every town.

There were other stories that month.

Hana talked on the podcast about a **biomedical** study at the hospital. It tested a new medicine for pain against a **placebo**: a pill with no medicine in it. "Some patients felt better even with the placebo," she said. "The mind is powerful. That's why studies must be careful."

A national newspaper was caught publishing a **fabricated** story: a journalist had invented interviews. The scandal **provoked** anger across the country. "This is why trust matters," Minh said on the podcast. "One fake story damages every journalist."

Andrei, at The Blue Cup, had taken on an apprentice: a sixteen-year-old boy who wanted to be a chef. "An **apprenticeship** is the best way to learn," said Andrei. "I wish I'd had one at his age." The boy **aspired** to open his own restaurant. "With **persistence**, he will," said Andrei.

Leo's sanctuary got money from a national **syndicate** of animal charities. "We can **equip** the clinic properly now," he said. "New machines, new medicine." He was going to **oversee** the new clinic.

Sophie Clarke, the archaeologist, found a beautiful **carving** at the dig: a small stone figure of a river god. "It's the best artefact we've found," she said. "It will **uncover** a lot about the people's beliefs."

Helen Ward talked about Dorothy Webb's old articles on space. "She wrote about **terrestrial** and space science. She imagined the first moon landing ten years before it happened. She wrote a blueprint for how it might work. Some of her ideas were **speculative**. Some were right."

A local scientist talked about rocket science on the podcast: how a rocket is **propelled** into space. "It pushes gas backwards very fast," he said. "Like a balloon."

Minh also had an **existential** moment that month. Rose Mai, nearly two, asked him a question for the first time: "Papa, why?" She asked it about everything: why is the sky blue, why does the dog bark, why is Grandma Rose old. Minh didn't know most of the answers.

"That's the most important question," said Hana. "Why. Never stop asking it."

There was one **aggravated** customer at The Blue Cup that month: a man who complained his coffee was cold, then hot, then too strong. Daniel was patient. "Some people just need to complain," he said. "Give them a **wholesome** cake and they **dissolve**." The man left with a smile.

The month's news **cascaded** quickly: one story led to another. Minh was **upfront** with readers: when the Gazette made a mistake, he wrote a correction immediately. He wanted to **empower** readers to trust and to question. A few readers sent **contradictory** comments: "too much good news," "too much bad news." Minh decided that meant the balance was right. He would **dedicate** the next month to one big project: the river.
