# This Week in Riverford: The Dig

Every month, Riverford Talks had a special episode called "This Week in Riverford": a round-up of local news, with short interviews. This one was full of surprises.

Story one: The dig. Builders working on the new youth centre found something under the ground: old stones, pieces of pottery and bones. Work stopped immediately. **Archaeology** teams from the university arrived. Minh interviewed the leader, Dr. Sophie Clarke, the underwater archaeologist, who was helping on land this time.

"This is an important **archaeological** site," she said. "We think it was a small settlement, about two thousand years old. We've found **artefacts**: pots, tools, a bronze ring. These people were our ancestors, in a way. The first people of Riverford."

"How do you know how old things are?" asked Minh.

"We classify the objects by type and compare them with others in our archive. Some styles are typical of one period. The Romans had a **colonial** presence here for four hundred years. Some of these objects are Roman. Others are older. It's a window into an ancient civilisation."

The builders were not happy about the delay. "It's a bottleneck," said the manager. "Every day we wait costs money. The council allocated the budget for six months. Now it's eight." But most people in the town were excited. Children came to watch the dig every day.

Story two: The fire. Two weeks ago, an old barn outside town burned down. The police said it was **arson**: someone started the fire on purpose. A young man was arrested. He was released on **bail** while the police investigated. Last week, at the trial, he was **acquitted**. The evidence was not strong enough. "**Circumstances** were difficult," said his lawyer. "He was near the barn, but so were other people."

The same week, there was an **assault** outside a pub: a fight between two men. One was injured. The police are investigating.

"Crime in Riverford is low," Inspector Shah told Minh. "But it hasn't disappeared. We're doing our best to **administer** justice fairly. Some people want us to be harder. Others want us to be softer. We try to be fair."

Story three: Business. A big shop in the high street went into bankruptcy after fifty years. Thirty people lost their jobs. At the same time, small businesses were having a boom: new cafés, craft shops and a vegan bakery had opened. "Small businesses are **burgeoning**," said Daniel. "Big chains are dying. It's a strange time."

A new billboard went up at the station, advertising a "miracle diet." Grace was angry. "It's **clickbait** on a wall," she said. "No food **additive** makes you thin. Don't believe it." The Gazette wrote a short article about it. The billboard was removed a week later.

Story four: Families. The council published a report on families in Riverford. More families now had both parents working; fewer had one **breadwinner**. More couples lived together without marrying; **cohabitation** had doubled in twenty years. More grandparents worked as **caregivers** for their grandchildren. The number of **adoptions** had grown, too.

Minh interviewed a woman who had adopted two children from Vietnam. "My children are **adventurous** and adaptable," she said. "They adapted to England so quickly. But I want them to know their roots. That's why I listen to your podcast."

Minh was touched. He invited her children to his Sunday club.

Story five: The language corner. Every episode, a listener sent a language question. This week's question: "What's the **antonym** of 'charismatic'?" Minh laughed. "Dull, perhaps. Boring." A **charismatic** person attracts others with their personality. "Grandma Rose is charismatic," he said. "Very."

Another listener asked about collocations: words that go together naturally. "We say 'make a decision,' not 'do a decision,'" Minh explained. "We say 'heavy rain,' not 'strong rain.' Collocations are hard, but they make your English sound natural."

At the end, Minh made his usual round-up comment. "This week, we found our ancestors under a building site, and we found our future in small businesses. We saw a court decision, a family survey and a strange billboard. Riverford is small, but it's never boring."

An **authoritative** history professor, cited often in the national press, sent Minh a message after the episode. "Your summary of the dig was accurate," he wrote. "Rare in the media." Minh printed it and gave it to Grace. She put it on her wall.

Later, Sophie explained more to Minh. "The **abolition** of the old way of digging, with shovels everywhere, changed archaeology," she said. "Now we use small tools and science. The **causation** of many changes in history can be found in small objects: a coin, a seed, a ring." She hoped the new findings would **ameliorate** the town's relationship with its past, after the difficult statue debate. "History can heal, too," she said. "It can **alleviate** old pain." The builders' anger slowly began to **abate** when they saw how excited the children were.
