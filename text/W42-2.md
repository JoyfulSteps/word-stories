# The First Wedding

In September, Minh and Hana got married in Japan. It was the first of three weddings.

The Japanese wedding was traditional. It took place at a Shinto shrine in Hana's hometown, by the sea. Only family attended the ceremony: Hana's family and Minh's parents and Lan, who had come from Vietnam and Tokyo.

Minh's parents were nervous. They didn't speak Japanese. They didn't know the customs. "What if we do something wrong?" said his mother. "What if we embarrass you?"

"You won't," said Minh. "Hana's family will help."

And they did. Hana's mother took Minh's mother by the hand and showed her everything. She explained each step with gestures and a little English. By the end, the two mothers were laughing together like old friends.

The ceremony was **profoundly** beautiful. It was quiet and slow, full of meaning. Hana wore a white kimono and a large white hood. Minh wore a black kimono. A priest performed the rituals. They drank sake from three small cups, three sips each. "It's for the **unprecedented** joining of two families," Hana explained later. "Well, not unprecedented. But special."

Minh had expected the ceremony to be **superficially** formal, a performance, and he had not wanted to worry **unduly** about it. But it was not. **Seemingly** simple actions had deep meaning. Every movement was careful and **intrinsically** meaningful. Every sip was a promise.

After the ceremony, there was a party at a restaurant by the harbor. Here, things were more relaxed. Hana's father gave a speech. He was not usually emotional. But he **vividly** described Hana as a little girl: stubborn, curious, always asking questions. "When she went to England, I was afraid," he said. "When she came back with a Vietnamese man, I was more afraid. But now I see. She chose well."

Hana cried. Minh cried. Both mothers cried.

Then Minh's father gave a speech in Vietnamese. Lan translated into Japanese. He spoke **explicitly** about his feelings, which was very unusual for him. "Six years ago, my son left home with one suitcase," he said. "I was proud and sad. Today, I am **wholeheartedly** happy. He has found a home. Not in England, not in Japan, not in Vietnam. In a person."

The party went on until late. There was a lot of food: sushi, tempura, grilled fish. Lan sang a Vietnamese song. Hana's younger sister sang a Japanese one. Then everyone tried karaoke, which was **invariably** terrible and **inevitably** funny.

At one point, Minh **inadvertently** knocked a glass of beer onto Hana's father. Everyone went silent. Minh's face went red. Then Hana's father started to laugh. "Now you're really family," he said. "In my family, everyone spills beer on me."

The weather was **volatile** that evening: one minute sunny, the next minute raining, then sunny again. "It's lucky," said Hana's grandmother. "Rain at a wedding means a long marriage."

"In Vietnam, too," said Minh's mother, through Lan. "And in England," said Minh, "it rains at every wedding, so every marriage is long."

There was one **stringent** rule at the Japanese wedding: no phones during the ceremony. Lan, who usually filmed everything, had to put hers away. Afterwards, she complained. "I have no photos! It's the most important day, and I have nothing!"

"You have your memory," said her mother. "That's better."

"Memories don't have filters," said Lan.

By the end of the night, Minh was exhausted but happy. The wedding had been **vastly** more emotional than he expected. He had thought of it as a formality. **Conversely**, it was the most important day of his life so far.

"How do you feel?" Hana asked, as they walked by the sea at midnight.

"Married," said Minh. "**Significantly** married."

Hana laughed. "Two more weddings to go."

"**Arguably**, one is enough," said Minh.

"Tell that to your mother," said Hana. "And Grandma Rose."

Minh **implicitly** understood: there was no escape. Three weddings it would be. The day had been **predominantly** happy, but it was not **trivial**. It was not **tedious** either, even the long ceremony. Its meaning was **tangible**: he could feel it, like the ring on his finger. Love, he thought, was not **ubiquitous** or easy. You had to choose it, again and again, with **vigorous** effort. But it was **viable**. **Subsequent** days would prove it.
