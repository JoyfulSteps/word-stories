# The Burglaries

In January, there was a wave of crime in Riverford. Five houses were broken into in two weeks. A **burglar** came at night, when people were sleeping, and took jewelry, laptops and money. The police said it was a **burglary** gang from the city.

Grace sent Minh to cover the story. He interviewed the victims. One old woman cried when she talked about it. "He took my wedding ring," she said. "It's not worth much money. But my husband gave it to me fifty years ago."

Minh felt angry. He wanted to help. But a journalist's job was to report, not to catch **criminals**.

The police were working hard. Minh talked to a detective called Inspector Shah. She explained the difference between crimes. "A burglary is when someone breaks into a building to steal. A **robbery** is when someone uses violence or threats. Theft is the general word for stealing. A **shoplifter** steals from shops. A **pickpocket** steals from your pocket in the street."

"Have you had any **murders** in Riverford?" asked Minh.

"The last **murder** was in 1987," said Inspector Shah. "The **murderer** is still in prison. Riverford is a safe town. Mostly. We have some **vandalism**, young people breaking windows and painting walls. Some **shoplifting**. A few years ago, there was a case where someone tried to **kidnap** a child, but it was a misunderstanding with a father. Mostly, it's small things."

Two weeks later, the police made an **arrest**. A young man was caught at three in the morning, climbing through a window. They found the stolen jewelry in his car. The old woman got her ring back. Minh wrote the story.

But then there was a surprise. The young man said he was **innocent**. He said he was climbing into his girlfriend's house, not stealing. And the jewelry was not his car; it was his brother's car. The police **charged** him with burglary anyway.

There was a **trial** at the court in the city. Minh went every day for a week. He had never been to a court. It was very formal. The judge wore a wig. There was a **jury** of twelve ordinary people who would decide if the man was guilty or not.

A **witness** said she saw the man in the street on the night of one of the burglaries. Another witness, the man's girlfriend, said he was with her all night. The lawyers argued. They **accused** each other of lying.

Minh found it fascinating and sad. The man was young, about his age. He looked frightened. His mother sat behind him every day. She was an immigrant from Eastern Europe. She told Minh, in the corridor, that her son had made mistakes but was not a thief. "People see a foreigner and think criminal," she said. "This is **discrimination**. It's **racism**."

Minh did not know what to believe. He wrote both sides carefully.

In the end, the jury decided that the man was guilty of two burglaries but not the others. His **punishment** was two years in prison. As a **prisoner**, he would have the chance to study and work. His mother cried in the corridor.

After the trial, Minh talked to Grace. "Was it unfair?" he asked. "Maybe he was **innocent** of some of it. Maybe people judged him because he's an immigrant."

"Maybe," said Grace. "The courts aren't perfect. That's why we report on them. Justice needs light."

Grace told him about the bigger problems in society: inequality between rich and poor, the **rights** of **refugees** and immigrants, homelessness in the city. "There's more **sexism** and racism than people think," she said. "And there's a **minority** of people who are **racist** and loud. But the **majority** of people are kind. Our job is to show both."

That evening, Minh walked past a homeless man sitting outside the train station. He had walked past him many times. Today, he stopped. He bought him a coffee and a sandwich and sat with him for ten minutes. The man's name was Pete. He had been a soldier. He had lost his job, then his house, then his family.

"Thank you," said Pete. "Most people don't see me."

Minh thought about equality, and human **rights**, and how easily anyone could fall. On the bus home, he wrote a note in his book: "Next story: Pete." He knew Grace would say yes.

In the **kingdom** of Britain, which was once an empire that ruled a quarter of the world, there were still people sleeping on the street. Someone had to elect to see them. Someone had to tell their story. The government could ban a lot of things, Grace said, and **punish** a lot of people. But it couldn't ban the truth. A **vandal** could break a window; a good story could open one.
