# Goodbye, Grandma Rose

Grandma Rose died on a morning in May, in her sleep, at home. She was ninety-five. The window was open. The garden was full of flowers. The river was shining in the sun.

Anna found her. She looked peaceful, Anna said, like she was just resting. On the table next to her bed, there was a letter. It said: "To my family. Read this after."

The family gathered that afternoon: Tom and Anna, Mia, Leo, Minh and Hana, and Rose Mai, who was five and didn't fully understand. Lan came from Tokyo two days later. Minh's parents flew from Vietnam. Hana's parents flew from Japan. Fiona came from Scotland.

Tom read the letter aloud. His voice shook.

"My darlings. If you're reading this, I've gone. Don't be too sad. I had ninety-five good years. More than most people. I want you to know some things.

Tom: you were a good son. Even when you were annoying. Look after your heart.

Anna: you were the daughter I never had. Thank you for putting up with me.

Mia: paint. Always paint. You have Margaret's gift.

Leo: look after your river. You turned out wonderfully. I'm sorry I said you'd be a terrible vet. You're the best.

Minh: you came as a guest and became my grandson. You gave me back Margaret. I'll never be able to thank you enough. Keep telling the truth.

Hana: you made Minh happy. That made me happy.

Little Rose: be brave. Be kind. Make my cake. And look at the stars through the spyglass. I'll be up there somewhere, telling the other stars what to do.

All my love, always. Rose."

Nobody spoke for a long time.

The funeral was a week later, in the old church by the river. It was full. Hundreds of people came: people from the town, from the Gazette, from The Blue Cup, from the podcast. People she had helped. People who had heard her voice on the radio. Strangers who had cried at "How to Live."

Many people spoke. Daniel talked about her chair by the window at The Blue Cup. Leo talked about the day she told him he would find a new dream. Mia talked about painting her portrait, the first painting she ever exhibited.

Minh spoke last. He had written a speech, but he didn't use it.

"When I arrived in England, I was nineteen and scared," he said. "Grandma Rose was waiting at the door. She put her hands on my shoulders and kissed my cheek and said: 'Now you are a member of this family.' I didn't believe her. I thought it was politeness. It wasn't. She meant it. For eleven years, she meant it every day."

He paused. "She taught me English, though she'd never call it that. She taught me how to make tea and how to say sorry. She taught me that pride is the enemy of love. She taught me that every family is built, not born. And she taught me to make the best cake in England."

People laughed through their tears.

"She would hate this speech," said Minh. "Too long, she'd say. Too sentimental. So I'll finish. Thank you, Grandma. For everything."

After the funeral, there was a gathering at The Blue Cup. Daniel served her cake. Her chair by the window had a small vase of roses and a card: "Reserved for Rose. Always."

Lan, who had become a linguist, gave a short speech too, in English, Vietnamese and Japanese. She talked about the **transcultural** family Grandma Rose had created, and the **interethnic** friendships she had made. "Some words get **mistranslated** between languages," she said. "But love never does."

Helen Ward said Grandma Rose had been one of the town's **forefathers**, or rather foremothers: one of the people who shaped its character. "Her **uprightness**, her honesty, was legendary," she said.

Minh was **cognisant**, that evening, that a chapter of his life had closed. He would never again hear her say "Are you eating enough?" But he would hear it in his head forever.

The Gazette published a special edition. Minh wrote a **dedicatory** front page: just her photo, her name, her dates, and one line: "She made us family."

There were small stories that month too, as there always are. A theatre company planned a **dramatisation** of her life. A **muckraking** tabloid tried to make a scandal about her will; nobody cared. A reader complained about an **imprecision** in the obituary's dates; Minh fixed it. Grandma Rose would have appreciated the **orderliness**.

Lan explained to Rose Mai, gently, what had happened. Rose Mai listened. Then she took the spyglass and went into the garden. She looked up at the sky for a long time.

"Which star is she?" she asked.

Minh knelt next to her. "Maybe that one," he said, pointing at the brightest one. "The bossy one."

Rose Mai looked through the spyglass. "She's telling the other stars what to do," she said seriously.

"Yes," said Minh. "I think she is."

Some people overrate fame and overvalue money, Grandma Rose had always said. She had neither. She had something better: a family that would remember her. Her **worldliness** was not about travel but about people. Even the **planetoid** that a local astronomer discovered that year, a tiny rock in space, was named after her by the university: "Rose." A small piece of the **macrocosm**, as Helen put it, carrying her name. Lan corrected Rose Mai's English that week, gently: "We say 'I'm going swimming': swimming is a **gerund**." Rose Mai said Grandma Rose would want her to use more **colloquialism**, not grammar. Everyone laughed. Even in grief, the house was full of words.

In the weeks after, Minh tried to **reawaken** the house's normal life. **Childrearing** didn't stop for grief. Rose Mai needed breakfast, school, stories. Life went on, as Grandma Rose would have wanted. There was no **contestation** about that. A **traineeship** for young carers was set up in her name by the hospital. A national charity offered Minh an **ambassadorship** for older people's rights. He accepted, for her. A **wrongdoer** who tried a **housebreaking** at the empty Gazette office that month was caught immediately; the town was watching out for its own. A report on **malnourishment** among the old, and on food with too much **monosodium** glutamate, was the Gazette's first story after the funeral. Her kind of story. The slow **reabsorption** of grief into everyday life had begun.
