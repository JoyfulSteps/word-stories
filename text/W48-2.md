# Riverford Talks, Episode 17: Writing Workshop (Part 4)

Minh: Welcome back. Sarah Lane is here again. Today: discussing data. In IELTS Task 1, students describe a chart. And in Task 2, they often use data to support an argument. Let's practise with a real topic: poverty and work.

Sarah: A serious topic. Good for practice.

Minh: Let's imagine a chart. It shows the percentage of people living at poverty-level in a country over twenty years. The line falls from twenty percent to about one-sixth, then rises again after an economic crisis.

Sarah: Good. First, describe the main trend. "Overall, the proportion of people living in poverty fell over the period, although it increased slightly at the end." Then give details with numbers. Don't explain every point. Choose the important ones.

Minh: What vocabulary is useful?

Sarah: For trends: rise, fall, increase, decrease, fluctuate. For groups: low-wage workers, low-skilled jobs, dual-income families, solo-parent households. For causes: rural-to-urban migration, the fastest-growing industries, government-funded programs.

Minh: Let's discuss the causes of poverty in the essay part.

Sarah: There are many. Low-wage jobs. Jobs with no-guaranteed-hours contracts, where workers don't know how much they'll earn each week. Non-completion of education. Low-prospect areas, where there are few jobs. Child-raising costs. And discrimination, which is why non-discrimination laws are important.

Minh: And solutions?

Sarah: Well-funded schools. Achievement-based scholarships. Dual-language education for immigrant children. Multi-purpose community centres, offering job training, childcare and advice. Problem-solving programs for young people. Well-maintained public transport, so people can reach jobs.

Minh: That's a lot of compound adjectives.

Sarah: This topic needs them. They make the writing precise. But again: don't overdo it.

Minh: How should students use statistics in Task 2?

Sarah: Carefully. Census-based data is reliable. User-generated data, from social media, is not. If you don't know the exact number, don't invent one. Examiners don't check, but inventing data is bad practice. Say "a significant number" instead.

Minh: What about examples?

Sarah: Use real ones if you know them. If not, use general examples: "In many developing countries..." That's fine.

Minh: Can you show us a good paragraph?

Sarah: "One major cause of poverty is the rise of low-skilled, low-wage employment. In many cities, workers are employed on contracts with no guaranteed hours, which makes it impossible to plan a budget. As a result, even dual-income families can struggle to pay rent. Government-funded training programs could help workers move into better-paid, high-frequency growth sectors such as technology and healthcare."

Minh: Excellent. Sarah, thank you.

After the episode, a group of policy-makers from the council contacted Minh. They had listened. "Your description of no-guaranteed-hours contracts is accurate," one wrote. "We'd like to talk about local solutions."

Minh invited them to the next episode, together with a single mother who worked two low-wage jobs. The conversation was difficult and honest. The mother described her life: up at five, two jobs, child-raising alone, no time for anything. The policy-makers listened. At the end, the council promised a new multi-purpose centre in the poorest part of town.

"Will they keep the promise?" asked Hana.

"We'll ask them every month," said Minh. "That's what journalism is for."

Meanwhile, the Sunday club had a new project. Students made a well-illustrated guide to essay writing, in dual-language versions: English and Vietnamese, English and Arabic, English and Spanish. It was cross-genre: part grammar book, part story collection. Mia designed the cover. It was well-organised and beautiful.

It was published online for free. In the first month, ten thousand people downloaded it. "A billion-dollar company couldn't make something this good," said Sam. "It's made with love."

Some of the essays in the guide were about technology: AI-driven tools, battery-powered cars, sun-generated energy, zero-carbon homes, zero-waste shops. Some were about society: anti-smoking laws, non-violent protest, gender-neutral language, bike-sharing in cities, traffic-restricted city centres. Some were about disasters: a student from an earthquake-affected region of Turkey wrote about rebuilding.

One essay, by a budget-conscious student who worked in a shop, was about an auto-oriented city that changed. Another, by a non-specialist in science, explained ultra-modern buildings in simple words. A third described a championship-winning football team made of refugees. A fourth was a sub-category story about lesser-used languages and why they matter. Each one was different. Together, they showed what the club was: people from everywhere, writing about the world.

Minh had one rule for the guide, which he wrote in the introduction: "No-exceptions rule: everyone's voice counts." Grace read it and said, "You've become an academic-friendly journalist. Or a journalist-friendly academic. I'm not sure which."
