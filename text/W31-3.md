# The Contract

In March, Minh got an anonymous email. It said: "Look at the council's contract for the new bridge. Ask who owns the building company." There was no name. Nothing else.

Minh showed it to Grace. "Anonymous tips are dangerous," she said. "Sometimes they're true. Sometimes someone wants revenge. We investigate, but we don't publish until we're sure."

Minh and Ellie started to dig. The council had given the contract for the new bridge to a company called Riverstone Building. The contract was worth four million pounds. Minh looked at the company's documents online. He wanted to identify the owners. At first, nothing looked strange.

Then Ellie noticed something. One of the owners of Riverstone had the same surname as a member of the council: a man called Mr. Hughes, who was on the committee that chose the company. "It could be a coincidence," said Ellie. "Hughes is a common name."

"Or it could be his brother," said Minh.

They checked. It was his brother.

This did not necessarily mean anything illegal. But council members were required to tell the council if a family member was involved in a contract. Mr. Hughes had not told anyone. "It implies that something is wrong," said Grace. "It doesn't prove it. We need more."

Minh and Ellie spent three weeks investigating. They talked to other building companies. Two of them said their offers were cheaper than Riverstone's, but they were rejected. They talked to a former worker at Riverstone, who **insisted** on staying anonymous. "Everyone knew the contract was theirs before it was announced," he said. "Mr. Hughes managed everything."

They also obtained the committee's meeting notes. Mr. Hughes had persuaded the other members to choose Riverstone. He said Riverstone had better experience. But Riverstone had never built a bridge before.

Grace read everything. "It's enough," she said. "But we must be fair. We have to ask Mr. Hughes for his side. He has the right to reply."

Minh called Mr. Hughes. He was polite at first. Then, when Minh explained the story, he became angry. "This is lies," he said. "If you publish this, I'll take you to court. I'll destroy your little newspaper." He threatened to call his lawyer.

Minh was scared. His hands were shaking when he put the phone down. "He threatened us," he told Grace.

"Of course he did," said Grace calmly. "Guilty people always do. That's not a reason to stop. It's a reason to be careful. Check everything twice. Then three times."

They checked everything three times. Their lawyer checked it too. Then, on a Thursday in April, the Gazette published the story: "Councillor Failed to Declare Brother's Company in Bridge Contract."

The reaction was immediate. The council suspended Mr. Hughes while it investigated. The national newspapers picked up the story. Minh's name was on every article. He got hundreds of messages, some kind, some angry.

A month later, the council's investigation revealed more. Mr. Hughes had helped his brother win three other contracts. He was forced to resign. The bridge contract was given to a different company, with more experience and a lower price. The town saved half a million pounds.

Grace took the whole team out for dinner. "To Minh and Ellie," she said. "Who survived threats, angry phone calls and three weeks of boring documents."

"I'm still scared of his lawyer," said Minh.

"His lawyer never called," said Grace. "Lawyers don't call when the story is true."

Later, Minh reflected on what he had learned. Journalism was not about being a hero. It was about **struggling** through boring details until you found the truth. It was about respecting the facts, separating what you know from what you **suspect**. It was about being bold but also accurate. And sometimes, you had to **tolerate** fear.

He wrote it all in a brief note in his diary. "Absolute truth is rare," he wrote. "But adequate truth, appropriate truth, is possible. Work hard enough, and the apparent becomes clear."

Then he went to sleep, and for the first time in three weeks, he slept well.

The next day, Grace sat with him in her office. "Let me tell you what this job is," she said. "Things occur every day in this town, and most of them nobody notices. Our job is to select the stories that matter and provide the facts. Not to impress anyone. Not to pretend we know more than we do. Never predict the end of a story before you know it. Never ignore a fact because it doesn't suit you, and never remove one because someone powerful asks you to."

"Powerful people will try to influence you," she went on. "They'll try to limit what you write. Some will expect you to **obey** them. You must oppose that, politely but firmly. People rely on us. They trust us to represent the truth, not one side. That trust is the only thing we **possess**."

"Did you intend to become an editor?" Minh asked.

Grace laughed. "No. I tend to interrupt people too much. A good reporter listens." Then she was serious again. "When you're tired and you want to settle for an easy answer, remind yourself: the easy answer rarely helps you solve anything. The alternative is to suffer later, when you're wrong. It's acceptable to be slow. It's not acceptable to be wrong."

She proposed a new rule for the newsroom: before any big story was released, two people had to check it. Her broad smile indicated she was proud of him. "Journalism can transform a town," she said. "Advanced technology, big budgets, none of that matters. Can you justify every word you wrote?"

"Yes," said Minh.

"Then you can sleep. Your story will remain true tomorrow. That should satisfy anyone."
