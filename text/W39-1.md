# The Attack

Yuko's new website was called Open Tokyo. In its first month, it had five thousand subscribers. In its second month, ten thousand. People liked its honest, careful stories. Then, in the third month, someone attacked it.

It happened at night. Ben, who did all the technology, got a message at three in the morning: the website was down. When he checked, he found malware: a dangerous program that someone had put on their server. It was sending readers to fake pages and stealing their passwords.

"It's cybercrime," said Ben the next morning. His eyes were red. "Someone broke through our firewall. Our cybersecurity was too weak."

"Who would do this?" asked Minh.

"Someone who doesn't like our stories," said Yuko.

Ben worked for three days without sleep. He had to troubleshoot the whole system. He found a bug in the website's code: a small mistake that let the attackers in. He had to debug everything. He configured a new server with a better configuration. He made sure all the readers' information was encrypted: turned into secret code that nobody could read. "Strong encryption," he said. "Now even if they get in, they can't read anything."

Minh didn't understand most of it. Ben tried to explain. "The old system was obsolete. Ten years old. It wasn't compatible with modern security. The compatibility problems made it weak. Plus, our broadband was cheap and slow. Not enough bandwidth to stop the attack."

They bought a new computer with a faster processor. Ben talked about **semiconductors**, silicon chips, gigabytes and megabytes. He connected new **peripheral** devices: a bigger screen, a backup drive. He made a new interface for the website: simpler, cleaner, with better resolution for photos, every pixel sharp. "In the old **analog** days," he said, "you couldn't hack a newspaper. You'd have to steal the paper."

There was a small glitch when they restarted, but Ben fixed it in an hour.

Meanwhile, Minh wrote a story about the attack. He was careful. He didn't know who had done it. He couldn't accuse anyone without proof. "That would be **libel**," said Yuko. "Writing something false that damages someone's reputation. They could take us to court. And saying it out loud is **slander**. Both are **defamation**."

Some big newspapers didn't care about these rules. A **mainstream** TV channel reported that "a foreign government" had attacked Open Tokyo, without any evidence. It was pure **sensationalism**: making a story more exciting than it really was. "That's how misinformation spreads," said Yuko. "One channel says it, another repeats it. Soon everyone believes it."

"And disinformation is worse," said Min-ji. "That's false information spread on purpose. **Propaganda**."

The attack became a big story. A famous TV **anchor** wanted to interview Yuko on the evening news. **Paparazzi** waited outside their small office, taking photos. A foreign **correspondent** from the BBC called Minh. "Can you give us an **exclusive**?" he asked. "A **scoop**?"

"No," said Minh. "Everyone gets the same facts."

Yuko agreed. "Our job is **objectivity**," she said. "We report the facts. We're not **subjective**, not emotional. We're **impartial**. We don't take sides. Even when we're the victim."

In the end, the police found the attackers: a small group paid by a company that Open Tokyo had criticized in a story about pollution. The company had wanted to stop the story. Instead, the attack made the story famous. Ten thousand new subscribers joined in a week.

"They wanted to silence us," said Yuko. "They made us louder."

Minh wrote a final column about it. He wrote about bias, about truth, and about the importance of independent journalism. "In a world of **sensational** headlines and fake stories," he wrote, "the most radical thing a newspaper can do is to be boring and correct." Grace shared it on the Gazette's website with one comment: "Exactly."

Ben slept for two days after that. When he woke up, he had a message from Yuko: a pay rise and a new title. Chief of Technology. "I'm the chief of one person," he said. "Myself." But he was smiling. The future of news, he said, was automation, robotics, maybe nanotechnology and biotechnology one day. "But the stories will still need people."
