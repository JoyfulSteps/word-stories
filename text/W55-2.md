# The River Report

In May, the Gazette published its biggest special report ever: "The State of the River." It was a year of work. Leo, Ffion, and three reporters had studied every part of the river: its water, its animals, its history, its future.

The report found good news and bad news.

The good news: the river was cleaner than it had been for a hundred years. The salmon had returned. There was a **resurgence** of wildlife: otters, kingfishers, rare insects. "The improvement is **markedly** better than we expected," said Ffion.

The bad news: there were still problems. **Untreated** sewage still entered the river after heavy rain, when the old sewers overflowed. **Pollutants** from farms washed into the water. Some side streams were **stagnant**: no movement, no oxygen, dead water.

The water company's **filtration** system was old. It sometimes **malfunctioned**. "The **ramifications** are serious," said Ffion. "When it fails, the river suffers for weeks."

The report also explained the **peat** in the hills above the town. "Peat bogs store huge amounts of carbon," said Ffion. "When they're damaged, the carbon is released. Protecting the peat is protecting the climate." She explained how **ultraviolet** light from the sun helps clean water naturally, too.

The report's history section told a dark story. Helen Ward had found an old **engraving** of the first mill on the river, built in 1780. Its **proprietor** had been a partner of Sir Edward Blake, the slave trader. "The mill used cotton grown by **enslaved** people in America," she said. "The river's wealth was built on their suffering. We should remember that."

The report ended with recommendations: fix the sewers, support farmers to reduce chemicals, protect the peat bogs, and create a river trust to **supervise** everything. "The river needs a guardian," said Leo.

The response was huge. The water company promised to invest twenty million pounds in new sewers. The council created the river trust. Leo was asked to be its first director.

"Me?" he said. "I'm twenty-six."

"You know the river better than anyone," said the mayor. "And people trust you."

It took some **persuasion**. Leo accepted. He was nervous. "What if I fail?"

"Then you learn," said Grandma Rose. "You've failed before. Remember your knee?"

There were other stories that month.

The hospital launched a new **regimen** for patients with heart disease, including exercise and diet. Tom was one of the first patients. "I'm the poster boy," he said. Literally: his photo was on the posters.

A storm caused an old wall by the castle to **crumble**. Workers repaired it **cautiously**, stone by stone, because it was historic.

The council had a long debate about the **periphery** of the town: the villages around it. "They feel forgotten," said James Morgan. "We must **enrich** their lives too: buses, internet, services."

The national government announced new rules on emergency **preparedness**: every town needed a flood plan. Riverford already had one. "We learned the hard way," said the mayor.

On the podcast, Minh interviewed a young woman who had arrived from Afghanistan as a refugee. Her **aspiration** was to become a doctor. "Everything **hinges** on my exams," she said. Minh's Sunday club helped her with English. She passed. Her story **illuminated** what many refugees experience. Some listeners accused Minh of **exaggeration**, and one called the story a **wrongful** attempt to make people feel guilty. He invited them to meet her. Nobody accepted.

At the end of the month, Minh had a meeting with the water company's director. She had been his **adversary** for months. Now they had to work together. "I didn't like your report," she said. "But it was fair. And it was right." She shook his hand. "Let's **bolster** this river together."

Minh **reassured** his team that it was a good sign. "Even enemies can become partners," he said. "If the truth is clear."
