# Lan's Classroom

In January, Lan began her teaching practice. For three months, she would teach English at a primary school in Tokyo, under the supervision of an experienced teacher. Minh went to watch her class one day, for an article about education.

The school was small and old. The children were eight years old, curious and loud. Lan stood at the front of the classroom, nervous but smiling.

Her supervisor, Mrs. Ito, had taught for thirty years. She told Minh her philosophy. "A teacher's job is not to fill children with information," she said. "It's to **foster** their curiosity. To **nurture** their love of learning. If you **impose** too many rules, you **inhibit** their creativity. If you **neglect** them, they lose interest. Balance is everything."

Lan's lesson was about animals. She didn't use a textbook. She used pictures, songs and a game. She **incorporated** movement into the lesson: the children had to act like the animals. Twenty eight-year-olds pretended to be elephants, snakes and monkeys. The noise was incredible.

"Is this normal?" Minh whispered to Mrs. Ito.

"It's excellent," said Mrs. Ito. "She **integrates** play and learning. Children learn best when they move."

But not everything went well. One boy, Kenji, didn't join in. He sat at the back with his arms crossed. Lan tried to include him, but he refused. "He's always like this," whispered a girl. "He doesn't like English."

After class, Lan was worried. "What should I do about Kenji?" she asked Mrs. Ito.

"Don't **exaggerate** the problem," said Mrs. Ito. "But don't **disregard** it either. Try to understand him first. What **hinders** him? What does he like?"

Lan talked to Kenji's mother. She learned that Kenji's father worked abroad, in America, and Kenji hadn't seen him for a year. "He thinks English took his father away," his mother said quietly.

Lan thought about it for a week. Then she had an idea. She asked Kenji to help her with a special project: making a video message in English for his father. Kenji was suspicious at first. But slowly, he began to participate. He learned words. He practiced sentences. At the end of two weeks, he made a video: "Hello, Dad. I miss you. I love you. Come home soon."

His father replied with a video of his own. Kenji watched it ten times. After that, he was the first to raise his hand in English class.

Minh wrote about it. He wanted to highlight what good teaching looks like. "A good teacher doesn't just implement a plan," he wrote. "She **perceives** each child as a person. She **distinguishes** between a child who can't learn and a child who won't learn, and she understands why."

He also wrote about problems in the education system. Mrs. Ito had a lot to say. "The government wants to **modify** the curriculum every five years," she said. "New ideas **emerge**, they **dominate** for a while, then disappear. Teachers can't keep up. And classes are too big. We want to **minimize** class sizes, but there's no money."

She talked about how the system could **discriminate** against some children. "Children who are different, foreign children, children with learning problems, are sometimes left behind. We must **intervene** early. Not wait until it's too late."

She also warned about the media. "Newspapers **distort** education stories," she said. "They **portray** teachers as lazy or bad. They **manipulate** statistics to make schools look terrible. You must be careful, young man."

"I'll try," said Minh.

His article was called "The Boy Who Hated English." It illustrated the power of one good teacher to change one child's life. Mrs. Ito read it and sent him a message: "You didn't **exceed** the truth. Thank you." And Lan, after reading it, said, "You made me sound better than I am." "No," said Minh. "I made you sound exactly as good as you are."

Lan's teaching practice **persisted** for two more months. Mrs. Ito said she had never seen a student teacher enhance a class so much. "She will establish herself as an excellent teacher," she wrote in her report. "She facilitates learning naturally. I want to ensure she gets a good job." The final report would exclude nothing; it was honest. Kenji initiated an English club at the school, with three friends. A small thing, but it would formulate his future.

Minh noticed something else. In everything Lan did, the idea of fairness seemed to **precede** everything else. Maybe that was the real lesson to **elaborate** on: good teachers make every child feel seen. That **induces** them to learn.
