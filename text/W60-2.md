# First Day

In September, Rose Mai started primary school. She was six and a half, confident, a little bossy, and very excited. She had her own small backpack and a lunch box with a Vietnamese rice ball, a Japanese rice ball and an English sandwich. "To be fair," she said.

Minh and Hana walked her to school. It was the same school where Minh had given a talk at Careers Day, twelve years earlier. Things had changed a lot.

The school was now net-zero: solar panels on the roof, a closed-loop water system that recycled rain, and a garden where children grew harvest-fresh vegetables. The playground had a self-sufficient small farm with chickens. The building was transit-oriented: most children came by bus, bike or on foot. Cars were banned near the gate.

The head teacher explained the school's values. "We're a merit-based school, but not a high-stakes one," she said. "No pressure, no zero-tolerance punishments. Children are self-assured, broad-minded, goal-oriented. We teach self-reliance and self-governance: children help make the school's rules."

"That sounds well-documented," said Hana, who had read the school's reports.

"Every bit of it," said the head teacher. "And the children are wide-ranging in their backgrounds. Thirty languages are spoken here."

Rose Mai was not afraid. She walked into her classroom and immediately started talking to a boy from Poland and a girl from Nigeria. Within ten minutes, she was teaching them a Vietnamese song.

"She's well-spoken for six," said the teacher.

"She's well-spoken in four languages," said Minh. "Code-mixing all the time."

At the gate, Hana cried a little. "She's growing up so fast."

"Too fast," said Minh.

That week, the Gazette published a far-reaching report on the future of Riverford: "Riverford in 2050." Minh's team had spent a year on it. They had talked to hundreds of people: scientists, planners, young people, old people.

The report's conclusions were wide-ranging. By 2050, the town aimed to be net-zero. It would be self-regulated, with a local energy company owned by residents. Its food would be more local: rain-fed farms in the hills, no imported vegetables in summer. Public transport would be star-powered, joked one engineer: meaning solar.

The report also warned of risks. Over-reliance on tourism could make the town fragile. Over-consume, and the river would suffer again. A shake-up in national politics could change everything. Some young people feared a going-nowhere future if jobs didn't grow.

There were deep-rooted problems too: inequality, housing, loneliness. "None of these will disappear by 2050," the report said. "But a self-governing, green-minded, fitness-minded community can face them."

The report was praised. A professor called it "the best local future study I've read." A national politician quoted it in a speech. A local critic called it "too optimistic," which Minh took as a compliment.

There were smaller stories too. A man was arrested for fire-raising in a farmer's field: a fire set on purpose. A rip-off company sold fake solar panels to old people; the Gazette exposed it. A state-sponsored hacking group from abroad attacked the council's computers; nothing was lost, thanks to a well-deserved upgrade the year before. A dot-com millionaire from London bought the old manor house and promised to open it to the public, opt-in for events, opt-out if residents complained.

A new council department, cross-functional and small, was created to manage climate plans. It had a revenue-raising plan: a small tourist tax. A high-yield green bond paid for the solar farm. A non-dominant party on the council, the Greens, had pushed for it. "Self-determination for small towns," said their leader. "We decide our future."

Lan visited from Tokyo and gave a talk at the school about e-transformation, how digital technology changes education. She explained that some ideas are self-evident: children learn by doing, by playing, by asking. "But other ideas need research," she said. "We test, we measure."

A semi-formal dinner at The Blue Cup celebrated the report. Daniel served a gravity-related dessert: a cake that looked like the planet Mars, in honour of the town's space instrument. Even Leo, who called himself a couch-potato on weekends, came.

At the end of the dinner, Minh spoke briefly. "Grandma Rose used to say: plant a tree. This report is a tree. We won't all see it grow. Our children will."

Rose Mai, sitting on Hana's lap, said loudly: "I'll see it. I'll be thirty-one in 2050."

Everyone laughed. "Then you'll write the next report," said Grace. "On a micro-market, a self-regard index, whatever new words they have by then."
