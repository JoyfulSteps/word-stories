# The Shrinking Country

In November, Yuko asked Minh to write a data story. "Japan's population is getting smaller," she said. "Every year. Find the numbers. Then find the people."

Minh spent a week with the data. Some of it was quantitative: numerical data, statistics, charts. Some was qualitative: interviews, opinions, stories. He needed both.

The numbers were shocking. Japan's population had reached its peak in 2008, at about 128 million. Since then, it had begun to **dwindle**. Every year, it fell by more than half a million people. "The **cumulative** effect over fifteen years is huge," Minh wrote. "Japan has lost more people than live in some whole countries."

The birth rate had **plummeted**. In the 1970s, Japanese women had about two children each, on average. Now, the figure was about 1.2. "This is not a **marginal** change," an expert told him. "The **magnitude** of it is enormous."

At the same time, the number of old people had **soared**. One in three Japanese people was over sixty-five. The number of people over a hundred had grown **exponentially**: from a few hundred in the 1960s to almost a hundred thousand today. "The growth has been **exponential**," said the expert. "Doubling, and doubling again."

Minh made a chart. He showed the **aggregate** population, the total, falling slowly. He showed the number of children falling fast and the number of old people rising fast. The change was not proportional. The difference between the two lines was **disproportionate**: there were not enough young people to care for the old.

He looked at regional data too. In Tokyo, the population was stable, even growing **marginally**. But in the countryside, villages were emptying. In some areas, the population had **plunged** by half in thirty years. "Tokyo is like a magnet," said the expert. "It pulls everyone in."

He learned some statistical words for his article. "The **median** age in Japan is forty-nine," he wrote. "That means half the people are older, half are younger. The **mode**, the most common age, is in the early seventies." He explained **percentiles**, **variance** and standard **deviation** in a short box for readers who liked numbers.

He also looked at the economy. "Every **quarterly** report shows the same thing," said an economist. "Fewer workers. Less growth. The costs of caring for old people **escalate** every year. The government publishes a **biannual** report, twice a year, and each one is worse than the last."

Some numbers had changed **substantially**; others very little. The **fluctuation** in the birth rate from year to year was **negligible**: tiny changes, but always down. There had been one small **surge** in births after a big government campaign, but it didn't last.

Then Minh went to find the people. He visited a village in the mountains where the school had closed. There was only one child left: a seven-year-old boy who took a bus for an hour to a school in the next town. "**Notwithstanding** the problems," said the village head, "we love this place. We won't leave."

He visited a nursing home where robots helped carry patients. "There are not enough human workers," said the manager. "So we use robots. **Thereby** we can care for more people." She showed him a robot that looked like a teddy bear. It could lift an old woman from her bed. "The robots have problems **therein**," she said, smiling. "They don't tell jokes."

He met a young couple who had just had a baby. "Everyone tells us we're heroes," said the father, laughing. "Our parents, our neighbors, even strangers. One baby, and suddenly we're famous."

His article ended with a question. "**Thereafter**, what?" he wrote. "What happens to a country when the young are few and the old are a **multitude**? Japan is the first to face this question. It won't be the last. Korea, China, Italy, Germany, even Vietnam will follow."

Yuko read it. "Very good," she said. "Clear numbers, real people. You've learned a lot."

"From you," said Minh.

"From everyone," said Yuko. "That's how it works." **Whereupon**, as was her habit, she returned to her desk without another word.
