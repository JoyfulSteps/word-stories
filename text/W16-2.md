# International Night

In September, the start of Minh's second year, the college held an International Night. Students from every country showed their culture, their food and their traditions. "Minh, you must do a table for Vietnam," said his teacher. "Wear something traditional."

Minh asked his mother to send him an áo dài, the long Vietnamese **costume** for special days, and some red paper **lanterns**. "In Vietnam, we celebrate the Mid-Autumn Festival with lanterns," he explained to the people at his table. "Children walk in a **parade** in the street at night, with lanterns of every color. It is a beautiful custom."

The hall was full of tables, flags, balloons and decorations. Students decorated their tables with pictures, food and music. It was like a **carnival**. Sam, as the host for the night, walked around with a microphone.

At the next table, a Scottish student named Callum wore a kilt. "**Scotland** is not England," he said seriously. "Don't call me English. My **nationality** is British, but I'm **Scottish** first." Next to him was a girl from **Ireland**. She talked about St. Patrick's Day and green clothes.

An American student, Jake, talked about **Halloween**. "In October, children wear a **costume** and a mask and go from house to house asking for sweets." Then he talked about **Thanksgiving**. "In November, the whole family eats a big dinner with turkey. We say thank you for everything." Tom came that evening and stood at Jake's table for an hour, eating pumpkin pie and talking about home.

A French girl talked about **Valentine**'s Day in Paris. "It's very romantic," she said. "In my opinion, the most romantic day of the year." Leo, who was with Tom, said, "It's the most boring day of the year." The French girl gave him a chocolate heart. He ate it immediately.

There was a table about religion too. A **Muslim** student from Pakistan told people about Ramadan and showed them a beautiful **prayer** mat. A **Buddhist** student from Thailand explained how people pray in the temples and give food to the monks. A **Christian** student from Nigeria talked about Christmas and Easter in her country. "In every religion, there are **holy** days," the teacher said. "Different gods, different ceremonies, but the same hope for peace."

There was even a wedding **ceremony** from India, acted by the students, with music and beautiful clothes. Everybody clapped.

Minh's table was very popular. Next to it, a student who worked as a translator had a table about languages. "Translation is not just changing words," she said. "You must understand the meaning. Every language has a different way to describe the world." She had a pile of books: a biography of a famous Vietnamese poet, an autobiography of a Japanese chef, and a novel with a beautiful cover that she helped publish in English. Minh had a long conversation with her about his own accent. They wanted to discuss more, so they agreed to meet for a longer discussion and a description of her job next week. People loved the lanterns and the spring rolls. Many people asked him questions about **Asia**. "Is Vietnam near China?" "Do people eat with chopsticks?" "Is it hot all year?" An old man asked, "Is it true that **Asian** students study more than **European** students?"

"I don't know," Minh laughed. "Maybe. But European students have more fun."

Some students talked about **Africa**. A Kenyan girl said, "People think **Africa** is one country. It's not! It has more than fifty countries. Every **African** country is different." Minh thought it was the same for Asia. People often thought **Europe** was the center of the world, too.

At the end of the night, there was a big celebration. The students from every country went outside. They lit their lanterns and walked together by the river. Then there were fireworks over the castle, red, green, gold and silver. Everybody watched the firework show together.

"This is like a family," said Sam. "All these countries, one night."

Minh looked at the lanterns on the water. He thought of his mother. He sent her a photo. "Happy Mid-Autumn, Mum," he wrote. "I'm wearing the áo dài." It was nearly their anniversary of his leaving home: one year since he left Vietnam. Was he a guest here now, or a resident? He did not know. Maybe both.

His mother replied: "You look handsome. But you need to eat more."
