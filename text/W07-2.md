# Goodbye

The last week before England was the busiest week of Minh's life. There were so many things to do. He needed a new passport photo, a bank card, warm clothes and a special bag for his laptop. His suitcase was on the bedroom floor, open and empty, for days.

"What do I take?" he asked his mother. "I don't know what is important. Packing is the worst job. It's boring, and it's worse than homework."

His mother was ready. She had a list. Of course she had a list. Every problem in her life had a list. It was the best idea she ever had, she said. "Clothes for cold weather. Medicine. Photos of the family. A piece of home." She gave him a small bag of tea and a little wooden box. "For when you miss us," she said. "For example, on your first cold night. That's the reason I'm giving it to you. Put it at the bottom of the bag."

The suitcase was heavy. Very heavy. "Is it a suitcase or a car?" Dad said, lifting it. "Maybe you should take less."

"Maybe," Minh said. But he took everything anyway. Each part of the suitcase had its own type of thing, all sorts of things: clothes at one end, books at the other, and quite a lot of food in the middle.

On the last evening, the family had a special dinner. Grandma cooked all of Minh's favorite food. Everything was perfect. The fish was lovely, the soup was wonderful, the rice was soft. Nobody ate very much.

Lan was quiet all evening. Usually she was the loudest and most noisy person in the house. Tonight, she did not say a word. After dinner, Minh found her alone at the top of the stairs.

"What's wrong?" he asked.

"Nothing," she said. Then: "You're going away. Who will help me with my English homework?"

"I'll call you. Every week. I promise."

"It's not the same," she said. "It's different on the phone."

Minh sat down next to her. "Listen," he said. "You're strong. You're not weak. You're the smartest, funniest person I know. Maybe even better than me. One day, maybe you will go somewhere too."

She almost smiled. "Really?"

"Really. Perhaps we will go together."

In the morning, the whole family went to the airport. Uncle Hai drove them in his truck. At the airport, there were hundreds of people. Some were happy. Some were crying. It was busy, loud and a little terrible.

Dad did not say much. He just held Minh's shoulder. "Be safe," he said. "Big cities can be dangerous at night. That's my only point." "Work hard. And if it's too difficult, you can always come home. There's no shame in that."

Mum gave him a big hug. "Are you sure you have your passport? Your ticket? Your money? Your phone?"

"Yes, Mum. Yes. Yes. Yes."

Grandma held his face in her soft hands. "You were a little boy just yesterday," she said. "Now you are a man. Go. See the world. Then come back and tell me about it."

Then it was time. Minh walked away from them, slowly at first, then quickly. At the side of the gate, he turned around one last time. They were still there, at the front of the crowd, in the middle of all those strangers. Mum, Dad, Grandma, and Lan, waving and waving.

He waved back. "Goodbye," he said. "Thank you. Please take care."

He was famous in his town now, a popular boy with a special scholarship. But here, he was just a young man alone with a heavy bag. He felt like a real adult for the first time, and also like a very small boy.

On the plane, he looked at the T-shirt he was wearing. Lan gave it to him that morning. On the back, in big letters, she wrote in English: "Hello, England. This is my brother. Be nice to him."

Minh laughed out loud. Then he cried a little. Then he slept. When he woke up, the plane was already over the sea, on its way to a small town by a river, with an old castle on a hill, where a family he did not know yet was waiting for him at the station.
