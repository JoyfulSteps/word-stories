# The Careers Fair

In November, the university had a big careers fair. Hundreds of companies came to **recruit** students. It was the biggest **recruitment** event of the year. There were banks, technology companies, schools, hospitals and newspapers. Every final-year student went. Every **candidate** wore a suit and carried a pile of CVs.

Minh went with Hana and a friend from his course, Tariq. Tariq wanted to be a teacher. "I want to work in a **state** school, not a private one," he said. "Everyone deserves a good education, not just rich kids."

They stopped at a table for teachers. A woman talked to Tariq about different kinds of schools. "There are **boarding** schools, where children live at school. There are **mixed** schools for boys and girls, and single-sex schools. There are **vocational** schools that teach practical skills, like building or cooking."

"What's the biggest problem in schools today?" asked Tariq.

"**Literacy** and **numeracy**," she said. "Too many children leave school without good reading and maths skills. And behaviour. Some children are so difficult that schools have to **expel** them. That's never a good solution."

Tariq took a **handout** and a **worksheet** about teacher training. "I'll apply," he said.

Hana went to the hospital table. They talked about nursing jobs and further study. "You could do a **bachelor**'s degree in nursing, then a **master**'s in a special area," the woman said. "Some nurses even do a **doctorate**. A **PhD** in nursing research."

Minh walked around alone. He saw a table for a big bank. They wanted business graduates. The salary was high. "Experienced staff earn a lot," said the man at the table. "Of course, you start at the bottom." Minh took a brochure but didn't feel excited.

Then he saw a small table in the corner. It said: "The Riverford Gazette." There was nobody there except one woman, sitting alone with a **textbook** about journalism and a **workbook** of notes. She was about fifty, with short gray hair and sharp eyes.

"Hello," said Minh. "Are you from the Gazette?"

"I'm the editor," she said. "Grace Okafor. Nobody comes to my table. Students want to work for big companies. Local newspapers are not fashionable."

"Okafor?" said Minh. "My landlord is Mr. Okafor."

She laughed. "Then you live with my father. He told me about you. The Vietnamese boy who fixes things and writes food reviews."

Minh couldn't believe it. They talked for half an hour. Grace told him about her **occupation**. "Being a journalist is not a job," she said. "It's a life. You work long hours. You're never rich. But you tell the truth, and sometimes, the truth changes things."

She told him that the Gazette was looking for an intern. "Part-time, during your final year. Then maybe a contract after you graduate. Maybe. Local newspapers are struggling. Employment is not guaranteed. There's a lot of unemployment in journalism right now. Many journalists work freelance. Being a freelancer is hard."

"I'd like to apply," said Minh.

"Send me your CV," she said. "Or resume, as Americans say. Two **references**. And three things you've written. I'll look at them. But I'm a hard **applicant** to please. I mean, a hard employer to please."

That night, Minh worked on his application. He chose three pieces: his review of the chip shop, his article about Science Week, and an article about saving The Blue Cup. He asked Dr. Bell and Daniel to write his references.

"Do you really want to work for a small local newspaper?" asked Tariq. "The bank pays four times more."

"I know," said Minh. "But I want to write. And I like small places. Big things happen in small places."

Before he sent it, he read Grace's company page. The Gazette was a hundred years old. Two years ago, the owners wanted to close it. Grace fought to keep it open. They had to **dismiss** half the staff. Some journalists were made redundant. Others had to **resign**. The old editor's **resignation** letter was famous in Riverford. He wrote: "I'm not leaving journalism. Journalism is leaving me."

Minh thought about the people who were **sacked**. He thought about The Blue Cup. It was the same story: small places fighting to survive.

He sent the application. Then he waited. He was good at waiting by now.

A week later, Grace called. "Come in on Monday," she said. "Let's talk properly. I don't **promote** people easily, and I don't take interns easily either. But your Blue Cup article was good. **Shift** starts at eight."
