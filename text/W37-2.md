# The Climate Conference

In October, there was an international climate conference in Tokyo. Thousands of scientists, politicians and journalists came from all over the world. Minh was one of the reporters.

The conference was huge and confusing. There were hundreds of talks, meetings and protests. Minh decided to focus on three questions: How bad is the problem? What can we do? Is there hope?

The first talk was frightening. A scientist from Germany showed the data. "Some changes are now **irreversible**," she said. "We can't undo them. Ice that melts in the Arctic won't come back in our lifetime. If temperatures rise more than two degrees, the results could be **catastrophic**: floods, droughts, hunger."

She talked about the causes. "**Overpopulation** is part of it. More people use more resources. But the bigger problem is the **exploitation** of nature. Rich countries **exploit** forests, oceans and land. They produce **hazardous** waste and every kind of **pollutant**."

The second talk was more hopeful. An engineer from Iceland talked about **renewables**. "Iceland gets almost all its energy from **geothermal** power: heat from inside the earth. And from **hydroelectric** power: energy from rivers and dams. It's possible to live without coal and oil."

A young woman from Kenya talked about plastic. "Most plastic is not **recyclable**," she said. "We must make less. Use less. In my country, plastic bags are illegal."

"What's the most important thing we can do?" a journalist asked.

"**Mitigation** and adaptation," said the engineer. "Mitigation means reducing the problem: less carbon, less pollution. Adaptation means preparing for the changes that will come anyway. We need both."

In the evening, the conference had a special event at the Tokyo planetarium. A famous professor of **astrophysics** gave a talk about the **cosmos**. "Look up," he said, as the dome above them filled with stars. "Our planet is a tiny blue dot in a huge universe. There's no other place we know of where we can live."

He showed them a **nebula**, a giant cloud of gas where new stars are born, glowing red and blue. He showed them the **constellations**: groups of stars with old names, like the hunter Orion. He showed a video of a solar **eclipse**, when the moon passes in front of the sun and the sky goes dark in the middle of the day.

"Light from the sun comes in a **spectrum** of colors," he said. "Red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet. We see only a small part. There's much more we can't see."

Then he talked about the smallest things in the universe. "Everything is made of atoms. And atoms are made of smaller **particles**: **protons** and **neutrons** in the middle, **electrons** around them. And even smaller things, studied in **quantum** physics, which nobody fully understands."

He talked about **isotopes**, different forms of the same element, and **radioactive** materials that give off energy. "This energy can light cities," he said. "Or destroy them. Science is a tool. It depends on us how we use it."

At the end of the talk, he talked about life. "Life on earth began with a single **organism**, very small, billions of years ago. Every living thing came from it. A chemical **catalyst** in the early oceans made the first **synthesis** of living material possible. We still don't know exactly how."

Minh walked out of the planetarium into the cold Tokyo night. Above the city lights, he could see only a few stars. But he knew they were there.

His article combined the conference and the planetarium. "We are small," he wrote. "But we're not powerless. The same science that shows us the danger also shows us the solutions. The question is whether we'll use them in time."

Yuko read it. "It's good," she said. "A little poetic."

"Grace would say sentimental," said Minh.

"Grace is right," said Yuko. "Publish it anyway."
