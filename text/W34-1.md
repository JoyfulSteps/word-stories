# Hana's Family

In April, Minh and Hana arrived in Japan. Before they moved into their small flat in Tokyo, they went to visit Hana's family in her hometown by the sea. Minh was very nervous. Meeting the parents of your girlfriend is hard in any country. In Japan, he had heard, it was harder.

Hana's family was big. There were her parents, her grandmother, two **siblings** (an older brother and a younger sister), her brother's **spouse**, and their two small children: a **toddler** who had just learned to walk and an **infant** of six months. There were also aunts, uncles, cousins and many people Minh thought were family but who were actually just **acquaintances** from the town.

Hana's grandmother was a **widow**. Her husband had died the year before. She was small and quiet, but everyone listened when she spoke. Minh bowed very low when he met her. She looked at him for a long time. Then she said something in Japanese. Everyone laughed.

"What did she say?" Minh whispered.

"She said you're too thin," said Hana. "Like all grandmothers."

Minh relaxed a little.

On the first evening, Hana's father showed Minh the family altar. There were photos of their **ancestors**, going back four generations. "We are the **descendants** of fishermen," said her father. Hana translated. "My great-grandfather was an **orphan**. He had no family. He built this house with his own hands. Everything we have, we **inherited** from him."

The grandfather who died last year had left the house to Hana's father, the oldest son. He was the **heir**. "In Japan, **kinship** is very important," Hana explained later. "Family is everything. Your **upbringing** is about duty to your family."

"Same in Vietnam," said Minh.

Hana's father asked Minh many questions. About his family, his job, his plans. Minh answered carefully. Then Hana's father asked: "Is your father a **widower**? I heard your grandmother lives with your family."

"No, my father's father died. My grandmother lives with us."

"Ah. Good. A family that takes care of old people is a good family."

Minh felt that he had passed a test.

The next day, Hana's father took Minh to an onsen, a Japanese hot spring bath. In Japan, people bathe together, without clothes, in hot natural water. Minh had never done this. He was very embarrassed. He tried to cover every part of his body: his **thighs**, his **torso**, his **buttocks**. Hana's father laughed and said something.

Later, Hana translated: "He said, 'Relax. We all have the same body.'"

In the hot water, Hana's father became friendlier. He showed Minh a long scar on his **forearm**. "From a fishing boat, thirty years ago," he said. Minh showed him the scar on his own knee, from falling off his father's motorbike as a child. They didn't have many words in common, but they laughed. With his **eyelids** half closed in the steam, Hana's father talked about Hana: when she was a child, her **adolescence**, her decision to go to England. "We were worried," he said. "She was a girl in a foreign country. Alone. But she came back strong. Now a woman. **Adulthood** suits her."

"She's the strongest person I know," said Minh.

Hana's father nodded. "And now she brings home a Vietnamese boy," he said. "Life is strange."

On the last night, the family had a big dinner. Hana's younger sister, who was sixteen, a typical **adolescent**, asked Minh a lot of questions about England. Her **peers** at school were all jealous, she said, because her sister had a foreign boyfriend. Minh was a **companion** for all the children; he played with the toddler all evening.

At the end of dinner, Hana's grandmother said something to Hana. Hana went red.

"What did she say?" asked Minh.

"She asked if you're my **fiance**," said Hana. "I said no. Not yet."

"Not yet?" said Minh.

Hana looked at him. "Not yet," she said again, and smiled.

Minh learned that night that Hana's aunt was a **stepmother** to two children from her husband's first marriage: a **stepson** and a **stepdaughter**. And Hana's cousin had a **stepfather** who was American. "Japanese families are more complicated than people think," Hana said. "Just like everyone else's."
