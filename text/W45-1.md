# Riverford Talks, Episode 7: Learning a Language (Part 1)

Minh: Welcome to Riverford Talks. This week and the next two weeks, we're doing a special series about learning languages. And I'm going to start with my own story, because many listeners have asked. My guest today is Hana, my wife, who also learned English as an adult. Welcome, Hana.

Hana: Hello! It's strange to be interviewed by my husband.

Minh: Let's start at the beginning. When I arrived in England seven years ago, my English was not good. I could read, but I couldn't understand people. I felt **helpless**. Do you remember your first weeks?

Hana: Very well. I felt stupid. In Japan, I was a good student. Here, I couldn't even order a coffee. The waiter asked me a question, and I didn't understand. I said "yes," and he brought me something with a strange **flavour**. I still don't know what it was.

Minh: I had the same problem. Every day, I made mistakes with a verb or a noun. I said "I am agree" for months. I forgot the plural of words. "Two child," "three sheep" (actually that one is correct). English plurals are very irregular.

Hana: And the **loneliness**. Nobody tells you about that. When you can't speak a language well, you're lonely, even in a crowd.

Minh: Yes. In my first month, I used to sit on the **rooftop** of the college library and look at the town. I felt far from everything. I could hear people talking, laughing, and I didn't understand the jokes.

Hana: How did you get through it?

Minh: The Parkers, mostly. They didn't let me hide. Grandma Rose made me talk every day. And Mia corrected my English, even when I didn't ask. It was annoying, but it helped.

Hana: For me, it was the hospital. You have to speak to patients. No choice. At first, I was so nervous. But patients were kind. Old people especially. They were patient with me. They were never in a hurry.

Minh: I want to talk about **optimism**. Many learners lose hope. They study for years and feel they're not improving. What would you say to them?

Hana: That progress is not a straight line. Sometimes your English goes **upward**, sometimes **downward**, sometimes it stays the same for months. Then suddenly, one day, you understand a film without subtitles. It's a **thrill**. That feeling is worth everything.

Minh: I agree. I also think motivation matters. You need a reason to learn. For me, it was the scholarship. And then, living here. You need to motivate yourself every day. Don't let bad days discourage you.

Hana: And practical tips. I used my phone a lot. I followed English accounts, read hashtag posts about things I liked. Nursing, cooking. I watched videos with a headset on the train. I made a simple template for writing emails, so I wasn't scared to write them.

Minh: I wrote everything by hand. My handwriting is terrible, but writing helped me remember. I had a notebook for new words: a word, a sentence, a picture.

Hana: And mistakes. You have to make mistakes **willingly**. If you're afraid of mistakes, you'll never speak.

Minh: That's the most important point, I think. Many students are afraid. They wait until their English is perfect. It never is.

Hana: Even native speakers make mistakes. Your neighbour, Mr. Okafor, told me he still confuses "affect" and "effect."

Minh: Everyone **confuses** those. Last question: how long did it take before you felt comfortable?

Hana: About two years. But it's still growing. It never stops. From now **onwards**, I'll keep learning until I'm old.

Minh: Me too. Thank you, Hana.

After this episode, Minh got hundreds of messages from learners all over the world. A student in Brazil wrote: "I thought I was the only one who felt lonely." A **grad** student in Korea wrote: "Thank you for being honest." A man in Egypt who was studying for an exam wrote that he had almost failed and **narrowly** passed on his third try. "Your story gave me hope," he wrote.

One message came from a young woman in Vietnam, eighteen years old, from a small town by a river. She was applying for a scholarship to England. She was afraid. "Can someone like me really do it?" she asked.

Minh wrote back himself. "Yes," he wrote. "Someone like you is exactly who can do it. Start today. Be kind to yourself. And don't wait for perfect."

He thought of himself at eighteen. He thought of Ms. Huong. Then he did something he had never done: he offered to help. He set up a free online club for Vietnamese students preparing for exams. Every Sunday, one hour, on video. He could **repay** some of the help he had received.

Grace thought it was a good idea. "Inexpensive and useful," she said. "Just don't let it disturb your podcast."

The club grew quickly. Soon there were fifty students. Some were very good; some were beginners. Some were inactive, joining once and disappearing. Others came every week. Minh saw them **regain** their confidence slowly. One shy girl, who never spoke in the first month, gave a five-minute talk in the third month. Everyone clapped.

"That's the best part of my week now," Minh told Hana. "Better than the podcast."

"Don't tell Grace," said Hana.

There was a sense of urgency in some of the students: exams were coming. Minh tried to make it relaxing, not stressful. "Relaxation helps memory," he told them, quoting Hana. He didn't want unrealistic goals. He wanted usable English.

Some things were not perfect. Some students misused grammar websites. One copied a whole essay from the internet. Minh explained why that was a bad idea, loosely but firmly. They also talked about chores, about family pressure, about **graffiti** on the walls of their schools, about life. It was not only an English class. It was a community. Some students would study peacefully on their own after class. Others refreshed their skills together. Many of them were mutually supportive, like brothers and sisters. Minh felt like a protector of something small and good.

Even the unpaid hours, he decided, were worth it. The eventual results would come. Maybe not tonnes of success. But some.
