# Cities

Minh's next article was about cities. He started in Tokyo, the biggest city in the world. Then, with Yuko's permission, he traveled to Manila in the Philippines, one of the most crowded.

Tokyo was clean, organized and enormous. Its **urbanization** had happened very fast after the war. Today, the **sprawl** of the city reached sixty kilometers in every direction. The center was **bustling** with people day and night. Some areas were so **densely** **populated** that apartments were the size of a parking space.

Minh walked along a wide **boulevard** lined with trees, and through a covered shopping **arcade** where old shops sold tea, rice crackers and kimonos. He walked down the main **thoroughfare** of Shinjuku, where three million people pass through the train station every day. Huge buildings rose on every side: towers of glass and steel, a modern **edifice** for every company.

But Tokyo had problems too. Some old neighborhoods were changing. Expensive cafés and new apartments replaced old shops. Old people who had lived there for decades had to leave because rents went up. "It's called **gentrification**," said a city planner Minh interviewed. "New money comes in. Old people go out. It's happening in every big city in the world."

The city used **zoning** to control what could be built where: houses here, factories there, shops in the middle. "Without zoning, cities become chaos," said the planner.

Then Minh flew to Manila. It was a different world. The city was **cosmopolitan** and full of energy, with people from everywhere. But it was also **overcrowded**. Next to modern towers, there were **slums**: areas where very poor people lived in small houses made of wood, metal and plastic. One huge **shantytown** was home to fifty thousand people, next to a river.

Minh visited with a local journalist, Carla. "People come from the countryside for work," she said. "There aren't enough homes. So they build their own. Without water, without electricity at first. Slowly, they become neighborhoods."

The traffic in Manila was terrible. Every evening, the main roads were in complete **gridlock**: nothing moved for an hour. Drivers took a **detour** through small streets. A police **diversion** sent cars around an accident. A **convoy** of government cars with flashing lights pushed through.

Carla showed Minh how drivers **navigate** the city. "No one uses **navigation** apps," she said. "They don't work here. You need instinct." Every **maneuver** was dangerous. Two motorbikes almost **collided** in front of them. A jeepney, a colorful local bus, had a **collision** with a taxi. Nobody was hurt. Everyone shouted.

"Is it always like this?" asked Minh.

"Every day," said Carla, laughing. "You get used to it."

In the evening, they walked along the **promenade** by Manila Bay, watching the sunset. Families sat on the sea wall. Children played. Old men fished. "This is the best part of the city," said Carla. "Everyone comes here. Rich, poor, everyone."

Minh thought about Hanoi, about Riverford, about all the places he had lived. Every city had a rich part and a poor part. Every city had a place where everyone came together.

Back in Tokyo, he talked to the city planner again. "Can big cities be fair?" he asked.

"They can be better," she said. "Good public transport. Affordable homes. Parks for everyone. A good police **precinct** in every **borough**. But a plan can **derail** easily. Politics, money, corruption. It's hard."

His article, "A Tale of Two Cities," was published on both sides of the world. In Riverford, Grace put it next to a story about the new houses in the town. "Big city problems, small town problems," she wrote. "Same problems, different size."
