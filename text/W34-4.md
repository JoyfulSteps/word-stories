# The Newsroom in Tokyo

To earn more money, Minh got a part-time job at an English-language newspaper in Tokyo. It was much bigger than the Gazette. There were a hundred journalists from twenty countries. The newsroom was loud, fast and full of interesting people.

His new editor was a woman called Yuko. She was **meticulous**: she checked every word, every comma, every fact. She was also very **decisive**. "Yes. No. Do it again," she said, in three seconds. Minh liked her immediately. She reminded him of Grace.

The other journalists were very different from each other.

There was Ben, an Australian **extrovert** who talked to everyone all day. He was very **extroverted**, but also **impulsive**: he once flew to Okinawa for a story without telling anyone. There was Aiko, an **introvert** from Osaka, who said almost nothing but wrote beautifully. "She's **introverted**, but she's not shy," Ben explained. "She just prefers to listen."

There was an old French journalist, Pierre, who was very **eccentric**. He wore a red scarf every day, even in summer, and talked to his plants. He was also the most **passionate** journalist in the office. When he talked about politics, his face went red and his hands flew everywhere.

There was a young American, Josh, who was very **naive**. He believed everything people told him. "He's **gullible**," said Ben. "Last week, I told him Mount Fuji was for sale. He tried to write a story about it."

There was a Korean journalist, Min-ji, who was **assertive** and **persistent**. When she wanted an interview, she called every day until the person said yes. "She's **resilient**," said Yuko. "She's been rejected a thousand times. She never gives up."

And there was a British man, Rupert, who was **vain**. He looked in the mirror every hour and checked his **hairstyle**. He had a **pierced** ear, a gold earring, and a big smile with a **dimple** in each cheek. "The **piercing** was a mistake from university," he said. "But I keep it. It's part of my look." He was also **temperamental**: happy one minute, angry the next. And **obstinate**: he never admitted he was wrong.

Minh tried to understand everyone. He was naturally **tactful** and diplomatic. When Rupert and Pierre argued about politics, Minh stayed out of it. When Josh made a mistake, Minh helped him fix it quietly. "You're very **compassionate**," said Aiko one day. It was the longest sentence she had ever said to him.

Yuko noticed too. "You're **conscientious**," she said. "And **diligent**. You work hard and you care about getting it right. That's rare."

"Thank you," said Minh. "But I'm also **indecisive**. I take too long to choose."

"Better slow and right than fast and wrong," said Yuko. "Ask Ben."

Ben, who had once published a story with the wrong country's name, laughed. "**Spontaneous**," he said. "I'm not wrong. I'm spontaneous."

In the first month, Minh learned a lot. He learned that the Tokyo newspaper worked very differently from the Gazette. Everything was faster. Stories came from the government, from big companies, from all over Asia. He wrote about trade, tourism and culture. But his favourite stories were still the small ones: an old man who had fixed bicycles in the same shop for sixty years, a school with only three students on a small island.

He also noticed something about his own **posture**. In England, he sat back in his chair. In Japan, everyone sat straight. After a month, he sat straight too. "You're becoming Japanese," said Hana.

"I'm becoming confused," said Minh. "Too many cultures in one person."

"That's not confusion," said Hana. "That's richness."
