# Riverford Talks, Episode 15: Writing Workshop (Part 2)

Minh: Welcome back to our writing workshop with Sarah Lane. Today's topic: technology and families. The question: "Technology has made family life better. Do you agree or disagree?"

Sarah: Another classic.

Minh: Let's start with ideas. How has technology made family life better?

Sarah: Communication, first. Families living apart can talk every day: video calls, messages. First-generation immigrants, for example, can see their parents on the other side of the world. In the past, they waited months for a letter.

Minh: I'm one of them. I call my parents every week.

Sarah: Exactly. Also, technology helps with practical things. On-demand services: food delivery, video-on-demand entertainment. App-based tools for planning family schedules. In two-income families, where both parents work, that's very useful.

Minh: And the negative side?

Sarah: Screens at dinner. Children playing single-player games alone in their rooms. Parents checking work emails at home. Less face-to-face time. And there's a between-generation gap: grandparents who don't understand the technology feel left out.

Minh: How should students structure their argument?

Sarah: They can agree, disagree, or partly agree. Partly agree is often the strongest, because it shows balanced thinking. Use cause-and-effect language: "Because of smartphones, families spend less time talking. As a result..." Examiners like cause-effect structures.

Minh: Let's look at vocabulary again. More compounds?

Sarah: Yes. Today's set: "high-pressure jobs," "high-risk behaviour," "risk-free," "risk-taking," "decision-making." "Low-income families," "middle-income," "high-income." "Small-scale," "city-wide," "community-led projects." "Self-driving cars." "Best-selling books." "Prime-time television."

Minh: That's useful. Any warnings?

Sarah: Don't translate word-for-word from your own language. Many students do this. It creates strange English. Think in English. And don't memorize full essays. Examiners recognise them.

Minh: Some students take a test-taking course that teaches tricks. Are tricks useful?

Sarah: Some are. Most aren't. The best preparation is real English. Read, write, speak, listen. There's no risk-free shortcut.

Minh: What about re-reading? Should students check their essays?

Sarah: Always. Leave five minutes. Check spelling, grammar, and whether you answered the question. Re-reading can save a band score.

Minh: Let's talk about one more topic, because listeners asked. Work and family. Many essays ask about the career-family balance.

Sarah: Yes. In a two-income family, the professional-personal balance is hard. Many parents work eight-hour days, plus commuting. Contract-based jobs and non-professional work can be insecure. Some parents become part-owners of small businesses to have more control. Others choose on-demand work through apps.

Minh: That connects to our earlier episode about work.

Sarah: Everything connects. That's what a good essay shows.

Minh: Sarah, thank you. This has been our most requested series.

The Sunday club wrote essays on the technology question. Minh read them all. Some were excellent. One student, a first-generation university student from a low-income family in the countryside, wrote about how a video call with his sister, who worked in a factory in another city, had changed his family's life. It was honest and moving. Minh asked if he could read it on the podcast. The student agreed.

When it was broadcast, many listeners wrote in. "This is the best essay I've ever heard," said one. "It's not about tricks. It's about life."

Minh learned something too. The early-stage ideas in many essays were good, but students were afraid to say what they really thought. They wrote what they thought examiners wanted. "Write what you believe," he told them. "With good structure and good English. That's enough."

He also noticed that internet-based learning worked well for some students and not for others. Some needed computer-based practice; others needed paper and pen. Single-language classes were easier, but multi-language groups were richer. The club had students who spoke Vietnamese, Arabic, Spanish and Chinese.

Hana, who listened to the episode, said she wished she'd had a club like that. "In Japan, my English classes were all grammar," she said. "Word-for-word translation. Very little speaking."

"That's why you're so good at grammar," said Minh.

"And so nervous speaking," said Hana.

There was one more nice moment. A well-designed food-related project from a student in Hai Phong, the third-largest city in Vietnam, won a national prize. She had used her English to research nature-based solutions for station-area pollution. She sent Minh a photo of her certificate. "Thank you," she wrote. "I couldn't have done it without the club."

Minh printed the photo and put it on the wall. Next to it, he put the first essay from the student whose sister worked in a factory. "These are my best-selling books," he joked. "Zero copies, but priceless."

Later, he read about a near-earth asteroid on the news, and a science-and-technology program on prime-time TV about it. He thought about how big the universe was, and how small his club. But small things mattered. A present-moment kindness, a non-physical gift of time, was sometimes the most valuable. Population-related problems, pressure-related stress, throw-away culture: the world had big problems. The club was a small answer. But it was an answer.

One student, a low-fat cookery fan, joked that the club was "the healthiest thing in her week." Another wanted a self-driving car. Everyone laughed. A part-owner of a café in Saigon offered to host the club's first in-person meeting. Minh accepted.
