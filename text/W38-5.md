# The Takeover

In September, Lan arrived in Tokyo. Minh and Hana met her at the airport. She was carrying two huge suitcases and a bag of food from their mother. "Mum says Japanese food isn't real food," she said. "Don't tell Hana."

"I can hear you," said Hana, laughing.

The same week, there was big news at Minh's newspaper. The paper was being bought by a huge **conglomerate**, a company that owned TV channels, magazines, hotels and a chain of convenience stores. It was not a merger of equals; it was an **acquisition**. The newspaper would become a **subsidiary** of the big company.

Yuko called a **briefing** for all the staff. Everyone was worried. "What does this mean for us?" asked Ben.

"I don't know yet," said Yuko honestly. "The new owners have sent a **memorandum**. They say they want to 'create **synergy**' between the newspaper and their TV channels. They want to use our stories on their websites. They talk about '**leverage**' and 'growth.'"

"That means they'll cut jobs," said Pierre.

"Maybe," said Yuko. "They've hired auditors to do an audit of our accounts. They'll look at everything."

Over the next weeks, the newspaper changed. New managers came. They talked about **benchmarks**, projections and a quota of articles per week. Every story needed to be "efficient." Every reporter had to write more, faster.

"It's a factory now," said Ben.

The new managers also wanted the newspaper to work with the conglomerate's other businesses. A story about a new restaurant? Mention that it was a **franchise** of the company's hotel chain. A story about shopping? Mention the convenience stores. "That's not journalism," said Yuko. "That's advertising."

There were also new rules. A new **regulatory** department checked **compliance** with company **regulations**. Every story had to be approved by three people. The **bureaucracy** was terrible. "It used to take one hour to publish a story," said Min-ji. "Now it takes one day. Everything is so **bureaucratic**."

Minh wrote a story about a new startup in Tokyo, a small company making cheap solar panels. It was an exciting **venture**: two young engineers with a great idea. But the new managers didn't like it. "One of our companies is a supplier of solar panels," they said. "This startup is a competitor. Don't write about them."

Minh couldn't believe it. He went to Yuko. "They're telling me what not to write," he said. "That's censorship."

Yuko was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "I know. I'm fighting it. But I don't know if I'll win."

Two weeks later, Yuko resigned. She wrote a short letter to the staff. "I can't work for a newspaper that serves a company instead of its readers," she wrote. "I'm sorry. I'll be starting something new. If any of you want to join me, call me."

Minh called her that night. "What are you starting?"

"A small independent news website," she said. "No big owners. No advertising from big companies. Readers pay a small subscription. It's a risk. The **feasibility** isn't certain. We'll need **liquidity**: enough cash to survive the first year. But I think there's a **consensus** among good journalists that something like this is needed."

"I'm in," said Minh.

"Think about it first," said Yuko. "It's less money. Less security."

"I don't need to think," said Minh. "I've done this before. I helped save a café with five hundred shareholders. I can help start a newspaper."

So Minh left the big newspaper too. So did Ben, Min-ji and Aiko. Pierre stayed; he was too old to start again, he said. But he promised to write for them sometimes.

Yuko's new website was small. It had no **logistics** department, no **procurement** office, no distributor or **wholesaler** for paper copies. Just six journalists, six laptops and one algorithm that Ben built to send the stories to readers. But it was free. Not free to read, free to write.

"It's like the Gazette," Minh told Grace on the phone. "Small, independent, poor."

"It sounds perfect," said Grace. "Every **monopoly** needs someone to fight it. Make sure you write something I can steal for Riverford."

Lan, who had been in Tokyo for one month, watched all of this with wide eyes. "Is your job always this dramatic?" she asked.

"Sometimes," said Minh. "Welcome to Tokyo."
