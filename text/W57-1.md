# Old Age

In April, Grandma Rose moved to the hospital's **geriatric** ward, which cares for very old patients. She was ninety-four. She had pneumonia. The doctors said she would recover, but slowly.

Minh visited every day. The ward was quiet and clean, but **impersonal**: white walls, machines, strangers. Grandma Rose hated it. "I want my chair," she said. "My window. My garden."

Hana, who knew the staff, made small changes. She brought photos from home: Margaret by the river, the family at Christmas, Rose Mai's drawings. She brought Grandma Rose's old shawl. She brought a **memento** from Margaret's cottage: a small painted stone. The room felt warmer.

Minh made a podcast episode about care for old people. He interviewed the ward's doctor, a calm woman with thirty years' experience.

"Old age is not a disease," she said. "But many old people are **sufferers** of several illnesses at once. Heart, lungs, memory. Our job is not just to treat illness. It's to give people dignity."

She talked about problems. "Too few staff. Too many patients. Sometimes care is **cursory**: rushed, quick. That's not good enough. Every patient deserves time."

She talked about loneliness. "Many old people here have no visitors. No family. That can **impair** recovery. Loneliness is as bad for health as smoking."

The episode led to something good. Many listeners volunteered to visit lonely patients. The hospital started a program: "One Hour a Week." Hundreds signed up.

There were other stories that month.

A political scandal hit the council. A senior councillor had given jobs to his relatives: his son, his niece, his brother-in-law. It was **nepotism**. Minh's team investigated with **painstaking** care. They checked every job, every document, and found people to **corroborate** the story. The councillor called it a **falsehood**. But the evidence was clear. The council voted to **oust** him. "It **exemplifies** what's wrong with small-town politics," wrote a reader. Minh disagreed in his editorial: "It exemplifies what's right: someone checked."

The councillor's response was **disingenuous**: he said he didn't know his relatives had applied. Nobody believed him. Some of his supporters, **hardline** and angry, tried to **topple** the Gazette's reputation online. It didn't work.

A national plan to build more care homes was finally **ratified** by parliament. "It's a start," said the geriatric doctor. "**Conceivably**, in ten years, we'll have enough."

Lan visited from Tokyo to see Grandma Rose. She talked on the podcast about how languages encode respect for old people. "In Vietnamese and Japanese, you use special words for elders," she said. "English doesn't. But respect isn't only in words."

The university announced that a small Riverford-built instrument would fly on a European space **orbiter** to Mars. A team of local students helped design it. "**Audacious**," said Ffion. "A small town on Mars."

Helen Ward talked about **oratory**: the art of great speeches. "Churchill, Martin Luther King, Mandela. Their words changed history." She played a recording of Grandma Rose's speech at Minh's wedding. "That's oratory too," she said. "Simple words, real feeling."

The codex experts made a discovery. A **cleft** in the old binding hid a small folded page. On it, the priest had written a list of the names of everyone who died in a plague year, 1430. The museum added it to its **depository** of historical records. "It's sad," said Helen. "But it's important. These people deserve to be remembered."

At the end of April, Grandma Rose came home. She was weak but happy. She sat in her chair by the window and looked at the garden, **teeming** with spring flowers. "That's better," she said. "Hospitals are for sick people. I'm just old."

Minh sat with her. She told him stories he'd heard a hundred times. He listened as if it were the first time. He wanted to embed every word in his memory. He knew, though nobody said it, that time was short. The **unproven** hope that she'd live forever was just that: a hope. He didn't want to **hasten** anything, or **confine** her to a bed. He just wanted to be there. He **mulled** over what he could do. The answer was simple: visit. Every day.

The decompression after a stressful month came slowly. One evening, Rose Mai fell asleep on Grandma Rose's lap. The two Roses, ninety-one years apart, slept together in the chair. Minh took a photo. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.
