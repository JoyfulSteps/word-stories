# The Sake Brewery

The sake brewery was three hours from Tokyo, in a small town in the mountains. It had been making sake for three hundred and fifty years. Minh went with a photographer.

The owner was an old man called Mr. Sato. He was the fifteenth generation of his family to run the brewery. He showed Minh around slowly, explaining everything.

"Sake is made from rice, water and a special mold," he said. "We **ferment** the rice for many weeks. The mold turns the rice into sugar, and then into alcohol. It's like making bread and beer **simultaneously**. Two processes at the same time."

He showed Minh the big wooden tanks where the rice was **fermenting**. The air smelled sweet and sour. "Every season is different," he said. "The rice is different. The water is different. The weather is different."

Next to the brewery, his wife had a small shop. She sold sake, but also vegetables that she **pickled** in the leftover rice from the brewery. "Nothing is wasted," she said. She also made a drink by **infusing** green tea with yuzu, a Japanese citrus fruit.

A famous sake **connoisseur** was visiting that day: a woman from Kyoto who had tasted thousands of sakes. With her was a French **sommelier**, an expert in wine, who was learning about sake. "In France, we think wine is the king of **gastronomy**," he said. "But sake is just as complex. Maybe more."

Mr. Sato took them to a small room at the back. It was full of old documents. "Here is the history of our family," he said. He showed them the records in **chronological** order, from the oldest to the newest. "The first record is from 1675. Every year since then, we have made sake. Three hundred and fifty **consecutive** years. No year missed."

"Not even during the war?" asked Minh.

"Even during the war. We made very little. But we made it."

He showed them a photo from the brewery's **centennial**, its hundredth birthday, in 1775, painted on silk. And the bicentennial, and the tricentennial. "Next year, our three hundred and fiftieth," he said. "A big celebration."

"Your family has seen many changes," said Minh.

Mr. Sato nodded. "Every **epoch** brings something new. The **bygone** days of the samurai. The opening of Japan to the world. The wars. Modern Tokyo. Each change felt **imminent** and terrible. Each one passed." He smiled. "Changes are **transient**. Only the rice is **perpetual**."

"What does the future look like?" asked Minh.

Mr. Sato was quiet for a moment. "**Hitherto**, my family has always had a son or daughter to take over. My son lives in Tokyo. He works with computers. He doesn't want the brewery." He paused. "For now, my nephew is the **interim** manager. But **henceforth**, I don't know."

Minh felt a **momentary** sadness. It was the same story he had heard in Riverford: old businesses, no one to continue them.

"The **preceding** generations didn't have this problem," said Mr. Sato. "But the **succeeding** generations have choices. That's good. It's just sad for old men like me."

He told Minh one more thing. "The biggest threat to sake is not young people. It's the weather. Climate change. Our rice needs cool nights. The summers are getting hotter. Last year, a **meteorologist** came to talk to us. She studies **meteorology**, the science of weather. She showed us the data: higher temperatures, less **precipitation** in some months, too much in others. The rice suffers."

Minh wrote it all down. That week, his column was not about food. It was about time: three hundred and fifty years, from **antiquity** to now, and what might come next.

He sent a copy to Mr. Sato with a **belated** thank-you letter, because he had been too busy to write sooner. Mr. Sato replied with a bottle of sake and a note: "**Timely** or not, thank you. Come to the anniversary." Minh decided he would.
