# The Earthquake

On a Tuesday afternoon in March, Minh was at his desk in the newsroom when the floor began to move.

At first, it was gentle, like a train passing. Then it grew stronger. The desks shook. Books fell from the shelves. The lights swung from the ceiling. "**Earthquake**!" someone shouted. Everyone went under their desks, as they had practiced.

It lasted about a minute. To Minh, it felt like an hour. When it stopped, the office was silent. Then, slowly, people came out from under the desks.

"Is everyone okay?" asked Yuko. Everyone was. A few things were broken. Nobody was hurt.

Minh's hands were shaking. He called Hana immediately. She was at the hospital. "I'm fine," she said. "We felt it. Everyone is fine. Are you okay?"

"I'm okay," said Minh. "Just scared."

"Welcome to Japan," said Hana softly.

Within minutes, the news came in. The earthquake was strong, but its center was far out at sea. There was a **tsunami** warning for the coast. Big waves could hit the shore. Yuko sent Minh and Ben to the newsroom's TV screens to follow the reports.

Minh watched the coast on the screen. He thought of Hana's family, in their small town by the sea. He called Hana again. "Your parents?"

"They've gone to the hills," said Hana. "Everyone has. It's the rule. When the earthquake comes, you go up."

The tsunami was small: less than a meter. It did no real damage. Hana's family came home that evening. But Minh would never forget that hour.

That night, he wrote about it. Yuko wanted a column about how Japan prepares for disasters. Minh talked to an expert at the university, a scientist who studied natural disasters.

"Japan is an **archipelago**," the scientist said. "Thousands of islands, sitting on the edge of four big pieces of the earth's surface. That's why we have so many earthquakes. And volcanoes: you can see the **crater** of Mount Fuji from Tokyo on a clear day. The last **eruption** was in 1707."

He talked about other disasters. A **landslide** after heavy rain, when a hillside falls down. An **avalanche** of snow in the mountains in winter. A **cyclone**, a big storm, which in this part of the world is called a typhoon.

Minh thought of Vietnam. "We have typhoons every year," he said. "And floods. In the **delta** in the south, where the Mekong River meets the sea, the water comes up every year."

"The **delta**, the **estuary**, the **lagoons** along the coast," said the scientist. "These are the most vulnerable places. Low land, near the sea. Climate change makes it worse."

He showed Minh a map of Japan's **terrain**: the **topography** of mountains, rivers and plains. There was a deep **gorge**, cut by a river over a million years. A narrow **ravine** in the mountains. A big river with many small **tributaries** flowing into it. A **basin** of flat land where Tokyo stood. A **wetland** near the coast, full of birds.

"Norway has **fjords**," he said. "Long, narrow bays between mountains. Japan has none. But we have everything else."

He also talked about weather. "Japan has every kind of weather," he said. "**Sweltering** hot summers, **sultry** and humid. **Torrential** rain in June. **Frosty** winters in the north. **Balmy** springs. **Inclement** weather on the coast. Some parts are almost **arid**, with very little rain. But most of Japan is **temperate**: not too hot, not too cold."

He showed Minh an old **barometer** on his wall. "My grandfather's. It measures air pressure. When the pressure falls quickly, a storm is coming. In the old days, fishermen used it to decide whether to go out."

Minh's column was called "Living on Shaking Ground." He wrote about the minute under his desk, about Hana's family running up the hill, about the scientist and his grandfather's barometer. He wrote: "In Japan, people don't pretend disasters won't happen. They prepare. Maybe the rest of the world can learn something."

Grace read it in England. She sent him a message: "Glad you're alive. Good column. Don't do that again."

"I'll tell the earth," Minh wrote back.
