# The Workers

In January, Minh got a message from a stranger. It was in Vietnamese. "Please help us. We are Vietnamese workers in Japan. We are being treated badly. Nobody listens."

Minh met the writer, a young man called Tuan, like his old friend. He was twenty-four and worked in a factory in a small town north of Tokyo. He had come to Japan on a work program, hoping to earn money for his family. But the reality was different.

"The agency in Vietnam took eight thousand dollars from my family," he said. "They promised a good job. When I came, they took my passport. I work fourteen hours a day. They pay me half of what they promised. If I complain, they say they will **deport** me."

Minh felt sick. He had heard stories like this before. But hearing it from a person, in his own language, was different.

He investigated for two months with Min-ji. They talked to thirty workers from Vietnam, Indonesia and the Philippines. They found a pattern. Some agencies were involved in human **trafficking**: moving people across borders and forcing them to work. Some were also involved in **smuggling** workers into the country illegally.

They found a lawyer who helped workers. She explained the law. "Taking someone's passport is an **offense**. Forcing someone to work is a **felony**: a serious crime. Paying less than the minimum wage is a **misdemeanor**: a smaller crime, but still a crime. The problem is that the workers are afraid. If they complain, they might lose their visa. **Deportation** is their biggest fear."

"Can they ask for **asylum**?" asked Minh.

"Asylum is for people escaping danger in their own country. These workers aren't refugees. They're migrant workers. They're **marginalized**: on the edge of society, without power or protection. Many **minorities** in Japan face this."

The lawyer had taken a case to court. The **plaintiff** was a group of Indonesian workers. The **defendant** was the factory owner. Minh went to the trial.

The **prosecutor** for the state presented the evidence. It was strong. The **prosecution** showed documents, photos and messages. The workers spoke about their experience. The factory owner said he didn't know what the agency was doing.

After two weeks, the judge gave the **verdict**. The factory owner was **convicted** of forcing people to work and stealing wages. His **conviction** was big news in Japan. He was taken into **custody** immediately. "Five years in prison," said the lawyer. "No **parole** for at least three."

"Will that stop others?" asked Minh.

"It's a **deterrent**," she said. "It shows other **offenders** that they can be punished. But one case isn't enough. The system needs to change."

Not everyone was happy. A week later, the agency boss was acquitted in a separate trial. To **acquit** someone means the court says they are not guilty. The court said there wasn't enough evidence. He walked out free. The police had **detained** him for a month, but now he was gone. "The **prosecuted** don't always pay," said the lawyer. "That's the hard truth."

Minh's story was published in Japanese and Vietnamese. It caused a big reaction in both countries. In Vietnam, the government promised to investigate the agencies. In Japan, an **activist** group started a campaign for workers' rights.

Tuan, the young worker, got a new job with a fair company. He sent Minh a photo: him and his friends, smiling, at a small Vietnamese restaurant in Tokyo. "Thank you, brother," he wrote.

The story made Minh think about **integration**. Japan was becoming more **multicultural**. More people of different **ethnicity** lived there: Vietnamese, Filipino, Brazilian, Nepalese. "But integration is slow," said the activist. "There's still **segregation**: foreigners live in separate areas, work separate jobs. **Ethnic** minorities don't have the same opportunities."

Minh thought about his own life. He had been a foreigner in England and now in Japan. But he had been lucky: a scholarship, a good family, good friends. Tuan had none of that. The difference between them was luck. Nothing else.
