# The Scholarship Fund

In February, the Sunday club had big news. Thanks to donations, it could now offer a **bursary**: a scholarship of money to help five students study abroad each year. The first winners were from Vietnam, Afghanistan, Brazil, Nigeria and Bangladesh.

Minh announced it on the podcast. "When I was eighteen, someone gave me a chance," he said. "Now we can give five people that chance every year."

The winners were **aspirational**: they dreamed big. A girl from Vietnam wanted to study medicine. A boy from Afghanistan wanted to study engineering. Their stories were moving. Many listeners donated more.

But there was a problem. One of the fund's volunteers was found to have taken money for himself: **misappropriation** of funds. It was a small amount, but serious. Minh was shocked and hurt. He had trusted the man.

He didn't hide it. He published the story in the Gazette. "We must be honest about our own mistakes," he wrote. "A fund that asks for trust must deserve it."

The volunteer claimed he was innocent. He said he had borrowed the money and planned to return it. The investigation took two months. It **exonerated** him partly: he had returned most of it. But he was removed from the fund. "Good intentions are not a precondition for good behaviour," said Grace, who advised Minh. "Systems are."

Minh changed the system. Now, every payment needed two signatures. Every month, accounts were published online. "No **vagueness**," he said. "Complete openness."

There were other stories that month.

The podcast did an episode on music. Priya, the pianist from The Blue Cup band, talked about the **timbre** of instruments: why a violin and a flute playing the same note sound different. "It's the character of the sound," she said. "Like a voice."

A car museum opened in the old factory. It had cars from every famous British **marque**: Jaguar, Rolls-Royce, Mini. Tom was very excited. "I could spend a week here," he said. He spent a day.

Hana talked about **prophylactic** medicine: medicine that prevents disease instead of treating it. "Vaccines are prophylactic. So is a healthy diet. Prevention is always better than cure."

A study showed that house prices in some London areas were **stratospheric**: ten million pounds for a small house. "It's a **quandary**," said Professor Ahmed. "Cities need workers, but workers can't afford to live there."

Leo's river trust used reprocessing of old plastic to build new benches by the river. "Waste becomes something useful," he said.

Lan talked about grammar: **subordination**, how one clause depends on another in a sentence. "'I stayed home because it rained.' 'Because it rained' is subordinate. It can't stand alone." She also talked about **specialisation** in linguistics: some linguists study sounds, some meaning, some history.

Ffion **postulated** a theory on the podcast: that the town's river had changed course three times in five thousand years. Sophie's dig supported it: they found old riverbeds under the town.

A **reticent** old man, who had lived alone for years, finally agreed to an interview. He had been a famous musician in the 1960s. Nobody in the town knew. "I didn't want attention," he said. "I wanted peace." His story was **unpretentious** and beautiful.

The council faced criticism for leaving many jobs unfilled: social workers, teachers, care workers. "There's a real inequity in pay," said James Morgan. "We can't optimise services without people."

Minh took Rose Mai on a little **jaunt** to the seaside one weekend. She was four. She saw the sea for the first time. She ran into the waves and screamed with joy. "Papa, it's so big!" she said. "Bigger than our river!"

"Every river goes to the sea," said Minh.

"Why?"

"Because that's where they belong," he said. He didn't know a better answer. Rose Mai seemed satisfied.

Grandma Rose listened to the story when they came back. She was in bed. "The sea," she said softly. "I went to the sea with your grandfather on our honeymoon... no, Margaret and I went. In 1958. We swam until our lips were blue."

She was confused about the details. Minh didn't correct her. Her memories were **corporeal**, physical, in her body: the cold water, the sun. That was what mattered.

The **connectedness** of everything struck Minh that night. Rivers, seas, people, stories. "We characterise life by its events," he wrote in his notebook. "But it's really characterised by its connections."

He was a **dutiful** son, Hana said, to two families. That was not **unquestionable**; it was proven every day. And no **despotic** illness would **vanquish** that. **Constriction** in Grandma Rose's chest made breathing hard some nights. The doctors were careful not to **overreach**. Her body was less **pliable** now, but her spirit was the same. The supposition that she would always be there was fading. But not yet.
