# Riverford Talks, Episode 3: Happiness

Minh: Welcome to Riverford Talks. Today's question is a big one: what makes people happy? My guests are Rose Parker, who is eighty-seven and has lived in Riverford all her life, and Leo Parker, her grandson, who is twenty-two and studies to be a vet. Welcome.

Rose: Thank you, dear. I've never been on a podcast. Is it like the radio?

Minh: Exactly like the radio. But people listen whenever they want.

Rose: How modern.

Leo: Hi, everyone.

Minh: Rose, let's start with you. What is happiness, in your opinion?

Rose: Oh, that's simple. Happiness is people. Family, friends, the people you love. Not money, not things. I've had a lot of money and very little money in my life, and I was happiest with very little.

Minh: Leo, do young people agree?

Leo: Partly. My generation thinks a lot about happiness. Maybe too much. There's a lot of pressure. On social media, everyone looks happy all the time. It creates expectations. You see everyone's perfect life and you think: why isn't mine like that?

Rose: In my day, we didn't have that. We had a telephone in the hall, and you could only use it for three minutes.

Leo: That sounds terrible, Grandma.

Rose: It was wonderful. Nobody could reach you. Silence was normal.

Minh: Leo, you had a hard time a few years ago. Your knee injury. Can you talk about that?

Leo: Sure. When I hurt my knee, I thought my life was over. Football was everything. I was deeply unhappy. I felt anger, sadness, everything. I stopped seeing people. I was in shock, really. It took a long time to understand that I could be happy without football.

Minh: What helped?

Leo: People. My family. Minh, actually. And finding something new. I found animals. Volunteering at a wildlife center. It gave me comfort. A new purpose.

Rose: He was a different boy after that. He came home every Saturday covered in mud and smiling.

Minh: Rose, you also had a difficult experience. Losing your friend Margaret for sixty years.

Rose: Yes. That was my own stupid fault. We had an argument when we were young, and I was too proud to say sorry. The consequences lasted sixty years. But then I found her again, thanks to you and Mia. We had two years together before she died. Those two years made me happier than the sixty before.

Minh: What did you learn from that?

Rose: That pride is a killer. It kills happiness. And that it's never too late. Even at eighty-two, you can fix something.

Minh: Scientists have studied happiness a lot. Leo, you study science. What do they say?

Leo: The research is interesting. Studies show that participants who have strong relationships live longer and report more happiness. Money matters, but only up to a point. Once you have enough for food, a home and safety, more money doesn't help much. Economics calls it diminishing returns.

Rose: I could have told them that for free.

Minh: What about everyday happiness? Small things?

Leo: Those matter a lot. Exercise, nature, sleep. A walk outside. Hunting for a nice view. Even laughing at a silly joke or a trick a dog does.

Rose: Tea. Don't forget tea. And a good chair by the window.

Minh: Last question. If you could tell young people one thing, what would it be?

Rose: Say sorry quickly. Say thank you often. And go outside. Don't sit in front of a screen all day.

Leo: Find what you love, and don't worry if it changes. My dream changed. My life is better because of it.

Minh: Rose and Leo Parker, thank you.

This episode became the most popular of the first season. Thousands of people listened. Many wrote to Rose. One woman wrote: "I called my sister after listening. We hadn't spoken for ten years." Rose kept every letter in a box. "Like Margaret," she said.

The response was worldwide. A **marine** biologist in Australia wrote that she had spent her life saving whales but had forgotten to call her own brother. A young man in the city wrote that he had left a **gang** and was trying to change his life; the episode had helped him. A mother wrote that her son, who had been ill for a year with strange symptoms, cried when he heard Leo's story. "You were an **angel** to us," she wrote to Leo. Leo went red when Minh read it out.

Not everyone liked it. One listener said Rose's advice about phones was old-fashioned and could do harm to young people who need their friends online. Rose replied: "Yes. I'm old. Thank you for noticing." The incident made the episode even more popular. It rose to the top rank of British podcasts for a week.

Soon, everyone wanted to be on the show. The owner of the small theatre wanted an episode about the arts. A group of junior doctors wanted one about the hospital. Someone wanted to talk about the old railway line, and someone else about a new smoking ban by the river. A school asked Minh to judge a writing contest. The format was simple, one question and two guests, but it seemed to capture something people needed.

Grace was pleased. The Gazette started a podcast membership club, and two thousand supporters joined in a month. "Your rating is higher than the BBC's local shows," she said. "Don't let it go to your head." Daniel joked that he wanted shared ownership of the show, since half the episodes were recorded in his café.

Minh tried not to chase numbers. He wanted the output to stay honest. He spent hours editing the audio, cutting long silences but keeping the laughter. Sometimes, in the surrounding quiet of the studio, late at night, he thought about whether he should quit. Then a letter would come from an unknown listener, and he would keep going.

Minh was surprised by the success. Successfully making a podcast, he decided, depends on one thing: being honest. He thought about his first week in England, when he felt **naked** and lost, with no words. Now he had a microphone and a whole town listening.

The next week, they recorded a live episode in the old town hall, a building with beautiful architecture and wooden walls the colour of honey, with visitors in the audience. People were attached to the show now. Afterwards, at closing time, Minh walked home by the river. The lights of the town were burning gold on the water. It was a beautiful sight.
