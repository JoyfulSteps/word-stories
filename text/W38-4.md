# Burnout

In July, Hana stopped sleeping.

It started slowly. She worked longer and longer hours at the hospital. There were not enough nurses, so she took extra shifts. She came home at midnight and left at six. She ate standing up. She checked her phone in bed for messages from work.

"You're a **workaholic**," said Minh one night.

"I'm not," said Hana. "I'm needed. There's a difference."

But one morning, she couldn't get out of bed. She wasn't sick. She was just empty. She cried for an hour and didn't know why.

Minh called the hospital and said she was ill. Then he called Dr. Tanaka, her boss. Dr. Tanaka came to their flat that afternoon. He talked to Hana for a long time.

"It's **burnout**," he said to Minh afterwards. "Very common in nurses. Too much work, too much stress, for too long. The body and the mind say: stop."

Hana took a month off. At first, she felt guilty. "My colleagues are working so hard. And I'm here, doing nothing."

"You're not doing nothing," said Minh. "You're healing."

During that month, they talked a lot about work. Minh realized he had the same problem, in a smaller way. He worked all the time too: at the newspaper, on his second book, on Lan's applications. He couldn't multitask anymore; he was doing too many things and none of them well.

They talked to friends about their jobs. Everybody had problems.

Ben, the Australian journalist, had been told his job might disappear. The newspaper was **downsizing**: cutting staff to save money. "Twenty people will be made redundant," he said. "**Redundancy** is a polite word for being fired. A layoff."

Aiko, the quiet writer, had decided to **telecommute**: work from home three days a week. "The company agreed to **flexitime**," she said. "I start at ten and finish at six. It's much better."

Min-ji had been approached by a **headhunter**, a person who finds workers for other companies. "A big American company wants to **headhunt** me," she said. "Double the salary. But I'd have to move to New York."

Pierre, the old French journalist, was on **probation** at his new job: his first three months were a test. "At sixty-two, after a twenty-year **tenure** at my old paper," he said. "Ridiculous. But that's the job market now."

Yuko talked about how the newspaper was changing. "Many companies now **outsource** work," she said. "They pay other companies, often in other countries, to do jobs cheaply. **Clerical** work, **administrative** work. Even some writing." She shook her head. "I worry about the future."

Minh asked Yuko about her own career. She was the most **competent** editor he had ever worked with, after Grace. "How do you avoid burnout?" he asked.

"I **delegate**," she said. "I can't do everything myself. I give tasks to other people. And I have a **mentor**: an old editor I call every month. She tells me when I'm working too hard."

"Do you think I could have a mentor?"

"You have one," said Yuko. "Grace. You call her every week."

Minh realized she was right.

He called Grace that night. He told her about Hana and about his own exhaustion. Grace listened. Then she said, "You know what I learned in thirty years of journalism? Work is not a **hierarchy** where you climb to the top and then rest. There's no top. The work never ends. So you have to rest along the way."

"How?"

"Choose what matters. Say no to the rest. Spend time with the people you love. Your **productivity** will go up, not down." She paused. "And Minh? Marry that girl before she gets tired of you."

Minh laughed. But he thought about it all night.

After a month, Hana went back to work. But things were different. She worked normal hours. She said no to extra shifts. In the **negotiation** with her boss, she asked for one day off every week, guaranteed. Dr. Tanaka agreed. "Your **competence** is more important than your hours," he said. "A tired nurse makes mistakes."

Hana was happier. So was Minh. They started walking in the park every Sunday morning, without phones. They cooked dinner together. They talked about the future.

"I think we've learned something," said Hana one Sunday.

"What?"

"That every **stakeholder** in our life matters: you, me, our families, our jobs. But we come first. Not the job."

"You sound like a business book," said Minh.

"I've been reading your **entrepreneurial** books," said Hana. "Too much **managerial** language. I'm going back to novels."

Minh laughed. He realized that his old **subordinate** role, always the junior, the intern, the student, was changing. He was becoming someone people asked for advice. It was strange. And a little scary. They had learned to collaborate, at home and at work, and their collaboration felt stronger than ever. **Mentoring** others, he decided, would be his next step.
