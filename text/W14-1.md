# Money Problems

In May, Minh had a problem: money. His scholarship paid for his classes and his room at the Parkers', but he had to pay for his food, his phone and his books himself. Every month, his parents sent a little money from Vietnam. He knew it was hard for them.

One evening, he sat at the kitchen table with a calculator and a piece of paper. He wanted to make a budget. He wrote down how much he could spend each week, and how much he needed to keep as saving. The total was not good. "I can't afford a new phone," he said. "I can't even afford new shoes."

Anna looked at his paper. "Let me see. Bank account, phone, books, bus... What's this fee?"

"The college library. I owe them five pounds for a late book."

"Students always owe something," Anna laughed. "Don't worry. The sum isn't big. But you need a small job. You could earn some money on weekends."

Minh knew she was right. But first, he had exams. The end of the year was coming, and every subject had a test. He wanted good grades. If he did not pass, he could lose his scholarship. If he got a low score, he could not go to university next year.

So he studied all the time. He went to the library every day. He used a big dictionary to check new words. On his desk, he had a ruler, a marker, a yellow highlighter, a pencil and a rubber. "In America, it's an eraser," Tom said, walking past. "I know," said Minh. He almost threw the rubber at him.

Meanwhile, Leo also needed money. His school was going on a trip to France, and the fee was three hundred pounds. "Can I borrow some money?" he asked his father. "I'll pay you back."

"Lend you three hundred pounds?" Tom said. "And how will you pay it back? With your salary as a football player?"

"I can wash cars," said Leo. "I can walk dogs. I'll do anything." Tom agreed to pay him a small **wage** for cleaning the garage every week.

Mia had her own project. There was an art competition at school, and the first prize was two hundred pounds. She needed new paints, brushes and paper. She bought everything online, because it was cheaper. When the box came, one paint was broken. She asked for a refund. The shop wanted her to exchange it instead, and she argued with them for a week.

One afternoon, Minh helped Mia's young friend Rosie, who was having trouble with maths. Rosie's mark in the last test was very low. She was eleven, and she had a pink schoolbag full of **crayons** and a pencil **sharpener** shaped like a cat. "I'm going to fail," she said. "I always fail."

"You won't fail," said Minh. "You just need practice. Let's compare this problem with the last one." After an hour, she understood. The next week, she got a good grade. Her mother gave Minh a box of chocolates and a note that said, "Thank you!" Rosie told every classmate about her new teacher.

"You should be a teacher," said Anna. "That's a valuable skill. Some people pay a lot of money for a tutor."

That night, Minh thought about it. He could help children with English and maths. That was something he could do. The next day, he went to the ATM, took out his last ten pounds, and bought a nice box of biscuits for the principal of Rosie's school, who agreed to put a note about him on the school board.

Tom laughed. "Very good. In my day, people paid with a **cheque** for this kind of thing, but nobody uses cheques anymore. Ask them to pay to your bank account, or by debit card. And remember to pay tax if you earn a lot."

"I won't earn a lot," said Minh.

"Every millionaire starts with ten pounds," said Tom. "Was the box of biscuits worth it? We'll see."

It was worth it. In a week, Minh had three students. His **headteacher** at college said it was a good project for his education, too. Every Saturday, he could deliver his lessons at the library during the break between his own classes. The amount of money was small, but it was his.
