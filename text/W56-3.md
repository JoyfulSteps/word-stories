# The Deluge

In November, it rained for ten days without stopping. A **deluge**. The river rose higher than ever. The flood defences, built after the great flood twelve years earlier, were tested.

They held. Mostly. The park on the floodplain filled with water, as planned. The houses were safe. But the roads were blocked, and the town came to a **standstill**. Schools closed. Buses stopped. **Vehicular** traffic was impossible in the centre.

Minh's team covered everything. He worked **tirelessly** for a week, sleeping a few hours a night. Hana was at the hospital. Rose Mai stayed with Anna and Tom.

The Gazette published updates every hour online. Where to get sandbags. Which roads were open. Which old people needed help. Volunteers checked on every elderly resident. "This is what community means," said the mayor.

When the water went down, the damage was less than the last flood. But some families had lost things. A few businesses had water in their cellars. The council promised to **reimburse** them for emergency costs.

Some families were in **arrears** with their rent before the flood. Now they were worse off. A charity offered help. Minh's article about them led to five thousand pounds of donations in two days.

There were other stories that month.

Leo explained the science of floods on the podcast. "Water from the hills **churns** down the river," he said. "In the past, forests slowed it down. Now we've cut the trees. So the water comes faster." The river trust planned to plant a million trees in the hills. "Nature and people must **coexist**," he said.

The council debated a new tax for flood defences. The **deliberation** took weeks. Some councillors wanted everyone to pay the same. Others wanted richer people to pay more. "An **egalitarian** system is fair," said James Morgan. "But it's not equal if poor people pay the same as rich people." In the end, they agreed on a compromise.

The theatre group staged a **melodrama** about the great flood of 1872: lots of crying, lost lovers, and a dramatic rescue. "It's not subtle," said Mia, who designed the set. "**Subtlety** is not the point. It's fun."

Hana talked about **willpower** on the podcast, after a listener asked how to stop eating sugar. "Willpower is like a muscle," she said. "It gets tired. Don't rely on it. Change your environment instead. Don't keep sweets in the house."

Lan, joining from Tokyo, talked about **coinage**: how new words are created. "Every year, dictionaries add new words," she said. "Some are very creative." She also gave advice for exam writing. "Don't copy sentences from the question. Paraphrase: say the same thing in different words."

Helen Ward talked about Riverford's **primordial** beginnings: the forests and marshes that existed before people came. "Ten thousand years ago, this valley was wild. Bears, wolves, beavers." She also talked about the history of the town's mixed population. "Riverford was always **heterogeneous**: Celts, Romans, Saxons, Vikings, Normans. And now Vietnamese, Polish, Pakistani, Nigerian. An **amalgamation** of everyone."

"Is racism **ingrained** in the town?" asked a listener.

"In some people, yes," said Helen. "But history shows that towns can change. Every generation has a chance."

Minh's book was finished. His publisher planned a **rollout** in spring: England first, then Vietnam and Japan. Minh dedicated it to Grandma Rose. "Without her," he wrote, "I would have been a visitor. She made me family."

He showed Grandma Rose the dedication. She read it slowly, with her magnifying glass. She didn't say anything for a long time. Then she said, "Don't **omit** the part about my cake."

"I didn't," said Minh. "It's on page forty."

"Good," said Grandma Rose. She was ninety-three, weak and tired. But her **contentment** was clear. Her **undivided** attention, when Minh visited, was still the best gift anyone could give him.

The **indulgent** grandmother of his early years in England was now his **revered** elder. He was a **pathfinder**, Helen said, for many immigrants in the town: someone who showed the way. His work was **purposeful**. And he felt a kind of **deliverance** from the fear he had arrived with ten years earlier. That, he thought, was the real story. A wildcat strike by bus drivers that month made the standstill worse, but even that ended. Everything does.
