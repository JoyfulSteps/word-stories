# What Do You Want to Be?

When Minh was sixteen, his English teacher, Ms. Huong, asked the class a question. "What job do you want in the future? Answer in English, please."

The classroom went quiet. Ms. Huong was a strict teacher, but she was the best teacher in the school. She wrote the question on the board in big letters.

One girl wanted to be a singer. Another wanted to be a dancer, an actor or an actress. A boy whose family had a market stall said, "I want to sell clothes, every size and every color." One boy wanted to be a football player. Tuan wanted to be a chef. "Because I love food," he said, and the class laughed. Another boy said, "A businessman. I want to be rich." A girl wanted to be a writer or an artist, and a quiet boy said, "A policeman, like my father." Someone else said "police officer" is better English, and Ms. Huong agreed.

Then it was Minh's turn. "I don't know," he said. "Maybe a driver. Or a farmer. Or a worker in a factory, like my uncle."

"That's not an answer," Ms. Huong said. "What do you love?"

Minh thought. "I like English," he said. "And I like learning about other countries."

After the lesson, Ms. Huong asked him to stay. "Minh, you are a good student," she said. "Your English is the best in the class. Did you know there are scholarships to study abroad? Some are in England."

"England?" Minh said. "But that is very expensive. My family is not rich. We are not poor, but we are not rich."

"A scholarship means the college pays," she said. "You need very good marks in every test and every exam. You need good maths, too, not only English. And you need to speak English very well."

That evening, Minh looked on the internet. He watched a video about a college in a small English town. He read the news about Vietnamese students in England. He read about the cost: thousands of pounds a year. In euros or dollars, the price was just as big. One pound was more than thirty thousand Vietnamese dong. For one coffee in London, you needed three pounds and some cents. Minh's eyes went wide.

Minh looked at the few coins in his pocket. "I want to travel, but even a cheap trip is expensive," he said to Dad. "The suitcase, the passport, the plane..."

Dad was quiet. Then he said, "You need it, so we will find the money. Don't think about the cost. Think about your homework."

From that day, Minh changed. He studied every night. He learned ten new English words every day. He asked Ms. Huong to teach him how to spell difficult words, and he read the English newspaper and English magazines at the library. When he was tired, he swam in the river or played a game on his mobile phone for ten minutes, then he started again. He did not take a vacation that summer.

Ms. Huong gave him extra lessons on Saturdays. She did not want any money. "You are my best **pupil**," she said. "When you go to England, send me an email."

"If I go," Minh said.

"When you go," she said. "Not if."

Many years later, Minh still remembered that meeting with Ms. Huong in the empty classroom. She was not his boss or his mother, but she changed his life. Art, music, history, sport: Minh liked all his classes. But only one teacher asked him what he loved.
