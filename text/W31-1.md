# The Numbers

In September, the government published new statistics about every town in England. Grace gave Minh the job of writing about Riverford. "Numbers tell stories," she said. "Find the story."

Minh spent two days with the data. He made a graph and a diagram for each topic. He also did his own small survey: he asked a hundred people in the market square five questions. Then he wrote his article. It was like a puzzle. Every statistic was a piece.

The first piece was population. Twenty years ago, Riverford had twelve thousand people. Now it had fifteen thousand. "There has been a steady increase," Minh wrote. "The population has grown steadily, by about one percent a year. **Whereas** many small towns have lost people, Riverford has grown."

The second piece was age. The number of young people had declined. The number of people over seventy had grown **sharply**. "The town is getting older," Minh wrote. "**Nevertheless**, young families are starting to move here from the city, because houses are cheaper."

The third piece was housing. Here, the numbers changed **dramatically**. Ten years ago, an average house in Riverford cost one hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Now it cost three hundred thousand. "House prices have doubled," Minh wrote. "Moreover, rents have risen **rapidly**. Therefore, many young people cannot afford to live in the town where they grew up."

He showed a line on the graph that went up and down. "Prices **fluctuated** between 2008 and 2012, then rose again. They have not been **stable** since." The growth was not gradual. Prices did not rise gradually; they jumped. It was fast.

The fourth piece was work. Unemployment had fallen. In ten years, it had almost **halved**, from eight percent to four point five. "This is good news," he wrote. "Furthermore, new businesses have opened, including cafés, shops and two small technology companies."

The fifth piece was the environment. The amount of rubbish going to landfill had decreased by thirty percent, thanks to more recycling. The reduction in car journeys was smaller. "**Hence**," he wrote, "Riverford still has work to do."

Grace read it carefully. She made many changes. "Too many numbers in one sentence," she said. "Readers will get lost. One number, one idea. **Regardless** of how interesting the data is, keep it simple."

Minh rewrote it. Then she read it again. "Better. But where's the human story? Numbers are cold. You need a face."

So Minh went back to the market square. He found a young woman called Chloe who worked at the bakery. She was twenty-six and still lived with her parents. "I can't afford a flat in Riverford," she said. "I'd have to move to the city, **amid** all the traffic and noise. But this is my home. My family is here. My friends are here."

He also found an old man, **amongst** the regular customers at The Blue Cup, who had lived in Riverford for eighty years. "The town has changed **beyond** recognition," he said. "When I was young, everybody knew everybody. Now, half the faces are new. That's not bad. It's just different."

Minh put these two faces **alongside** the numbers. **Underneath** each graph, he wrote one sentence from a real person. **Whilst** the numbers showed the big picture, the people showed what it meant.

"**Regarding** the housing crisis," he wrote at the end, "the council says it is building two hundred new homes. **Concerning** the price of those homes, it has not yet said. Thus the big question remains: who will be able to live in Riverford in twenty years?"

Grace read the final version. "Good," she said. "This is real journalism. Numbers with a heart. **Nonetheless**, you used 'moreover' three times. Nobody says 'moreover' in real life."

"My IELTS teacher said it was a good linking word," said Minh.

"Your IELTS teacher isn't my editor," said Grace. But she was smiling.

The article was shared widely. The council invited Minh to a meeting about housing. "We read your article," said the mayor. "A tonne of people did. Or a ton, if you're American. **Whereby** I mean: a lot." He laughed at his own joke. Nobody else did. The new homes plan was changed after that, **whereby** a third of the homes would be affordable for young people. A small victory, measured in cubic meters of concrete and a lot of words.
