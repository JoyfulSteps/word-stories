# The Rumor

The big story Grace talked about began with a **rumor**. Someone wrote on social media that the town council was going to close the Riverford hospital and move it to the city. The post went viral. In one day, ten thousand people shared it. It was trending on every app. People were angry and afraid.

"Is it true?" Grace asked at the morning meeting. "That's our job. Find out."

She sent Minh and Ellie to investigate. On the way out, Minh's old laptop began to freeze, as usual. He had to restart it twice and reboot the internet router. "Make a backup of everything," said Grace. "Always." The Gazette had no automatic system for saving files. "One day we'll automate it," she said. "Maybe an artificial intelligence will do my job. A virtual editor. Until then, I'm the editor here." "This is real **journalism**," she said. "Not cats in trees. Be careful. Check everything."

They started with the post. It came from an **influencer** with fifty thousand followers who usually posted about fashion. She didn't say where she got the information. "It might be fake," said Ellie. "Or it might be true. We need a source."

"A political scandal like this could be huge," said Ellie. "If it's true." They called the hospital. The press office said: "No comment." They called the council. "No announcement has been made," said a spokesperson. That was not a no.

Minh had an idea. "Hana works at the hospital. Maybe she's heard something."

Hana had heard something. "There's **gossip** in the staff room," she said. "Everyone's worried. But nobody knows anything official."

Ellie found a document on the council website: the minutes of a meeting from last month. It was very long and boring. But on page forty, there was a short sentence: "The committee discussed possible changes to local health services." That was all.

"It's not proof," said Grace. "But it's something."

They kept working. Minh went to a public meeting of the council. A politician stood up to answer questions. The room was full of angry people. "There are no plans to close the hospital," he said. "Not at this time."

"Not at this time?" said Minh, standing up. "Does that mean in the future?"

The politician looked uncomfortable. "I can't comment on the future."

The next day, the story was in every newspaper. The big national **tabloids** had dramatic headlines: "TOWN TO LOSE HOSPITAL!" The serious **broadsheet** papers were more careful. On TV, a **bulletin** on the evening news had a short report. A national **columnist** wrote that small towns were being forgotten by the government.

But the Gazette did something different. Grace wrote an **editorial**. She explained what was known and what was not known. "We don't publish rumors," she wrote. "We publish facts. Here are the facts. Here are the questions. We will keep asking until we get answers."

Readers loved it. The website had its biggest day ever. Hundreds of new subscribers signed up. Readers, viewers of the local TV news and listeners of local radio all quoted the Gazette.

Some people were angry, though. A local **critic** wrote that the Gazette was biased against the council. Others said the opposite. "If both sides are angry with you," said Grace, "you're probably doing a good job."

There was also a question of privacy. Ellie found the name of a nurse who said the hospital was closing. "Do we use her name?" asked Minh.

"No," said Grace firmly. "She'll lose her job. We protect our sources. Always."

A week later, the truth came out. The council had a plan, but not to close the hospital. They wanted to move one small department to the city. The influencer's post was exaggerated. But it wasn't completely false.

At the end of the week, Grace called Minh into her office. "You asked the right question at that meeting," she said. "'Does that mean in the future?' That one question made the whole story."

"I was just curious," said Minh.

"Curiosity is a journalist's best tool," said Grace. "Never lose it."

That evening, Minh thought about what he had learned. In Vietnam, newspapers were controlled by the government. There was **censorship**. Journalists could not **censor** themselves completely, but they had to be careful. Here, the press was free. In a **democracy**, the press could ask questions of a minister, a member of **parliament**, even the prime minister. That freedom was important. It came with responsibility, too.

He thought about politics and society. Britain was a **democratic** country, but also a **monarchy**, with a king. America was a **republic**, with a president and a **congress** with two parts, the House and the Senate. Some countries had a **dictator**. Every system was different. But in every system, he thought, people needed the truth.

He wrote it all in his notebook. Then he wrote a new column for the next edition of the Gazette. It was called "Questions." It was about why asking questions matters. The publisher of the Gazette, an old friend of Grace's, read it and said it was the best **commentary** of the year. Grace put it on the front page. That kind of coverage for an intern was unusual. Nobody, not even Grace, used the word criticism that week. It was all praise. The story was not reality TV or a sitcom. It was real.
