# The Exhibition

In January, the museum opened a big exhibition about medicine through the ages. Minh's newspaper was a partner. Helen Ward was asked to **curate** it: to choose the objects and tell the story.

The exhibition began with ancient medicine: herbs, prayers and strange treatments. Then it moved to the Middle Ages, when diseases like the plague killed millions. "Many diseases were **incurable**," said Helen. "People died of things we treat easily today."

The most famous section was about **penicillin**, the first antibiotic, discovered in 1928 by Alexander Fleming. "It changed everything," said Helen. "Infections that killed people became easy to treat. Millions of lives saved."

But the exhibition also had a warning. Hana, who helped with the medical information, explained it on the podcast. "Antibiotics are losing their power," she said. "Bacteria are becoming resistant. If we don't use antibiotics carefully, some infections could become incurable again."

She also talked about how medical knowledge spreads. "Scientists **disseminate** their discoveries through journals and conferences. But ordinary people often get wrong information online. Doctors must explain things clearly. No **lingo**, no jargon. Patients shouldn't have to decode what we say."

The exhibition was very popular. Schools sent classes. Families came on weekends. One section, about the **metamorphosis** of insects and how scientists studied them to understand cells, was especially loved by children.

There were other stories that month.

At Leo's sanctuary, the children's favourite event was watching butterflies emerge from their cocoons. "Metamorphosis is magic," said a seven-year-old. "But it's real."

The river trust installed **photovoltaic** solar panels on an old grain **silo** at a farm. The silo was empty now. "It's perfect," said Leo. "Tall, sunny, no shadows." The panels would power the trust's office and the visitor centre.

Ffion talked about energy on the podcast. "**Hydrocarbons**, oil and gas, are still the main fuels," she said. "But they're running out, and they cause climate change. We must replace them." She explained how water **vapour** in the air traps heat too.

The police **apprehended** a man flying an **unauthorised** drone over the hospital. It was a security risk. "There are rules for drones," said Inspector Shah. "Without **authorisation**, it's illegal near hospitals and airports."

The council faced a money **shortfall**: two million pounds less than expected. A council meeting to agree on cuts needed everyone's **assent**. It took three meetings. "Democracy is slow," said the mayor. "But it's fair."

A local school was praised for its results. Its students **outperformed** students from much richer schools. The head teacher received a **commendation** from the government. "Our students work hard," she said. "And they have great **backers**: their families."

Lan talked about a linguistic **conundrum** on the podcast: why some languages have many words for one thing. "Japanese has many words for rain," she said. "Vietnamese has many words for rice. English has many words for... well, rain too." She talked about how surveys can **skew** results if the questions are badly written.

Minh's Sunday club had grown to five thousand students worldwide. Volunteers ran it now. Minh only taught once a month. "The club doesn't need me anymore," he said. "That's success."

Hana disagreed. "It still needs you. Just less."

Helen Ward told the story of the **disintegration** of old empires and the **toil** of ordinary workers through history. "History is mostly about people working very hard for very little," she said. "We forget them."

Leo was asked to give a talk at a national conference. He was nervous. Before, he would have said no. Now, he was **intrepid**. "I'll do it," he said. "For the river."

The talk was a success. He spoke about the salmon, the trees, the volunteers. "Every person who helped is **irreplaceable**," he said. "Not one of them was paid. They did it for love."

When he came home, Grandma Rose was waiting. She was in bed most of the day now. "How did it go?" she asked.

"Good," said Leo. "People clapped."

"Of course they did," said Grandma Rose. "You're my grandson." She held his hand. Her grip was weaker, but still there. "Remember the knee?" she said. "You thought your life was over."

"I remember."

"Look at you now." She smiled. "Life has **ebbs** and flows. Like your river. The bad times go. The good times come back."

Leo didn't trust his voice. He just nodded.

A **cramp** in her leg woke her that night, but she was fine by morning. **Impotent** against age, the doctors said, but she was still herself. **Proportionate** care, not too much, not too little: that was the plan. The **uniformity** of her days didn't bother her. "Old age is boring," she said. "That's why I need visitors."
