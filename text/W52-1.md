# The Successor

In February, Grace called Minh into her office. She closed the door. That was never a good sign.

"Sit down," she said. "I have news. I'm retiring in June."

Minh was shocked. Grace had run the Gazette for fifteen years. He couldn't imagine it without her.

"Why?" he asked.

"I'm sixty-five. My father is ninety-four and needs me. And I'm tired." She smiled, which was rare. "And I've found my **successor**."

"Who?"

"You."

Minh couldn't speak. His gut told him he wasn't ready. "I'm not ready," he said.

"Nobody is ever ready," said Grace. "I wasn't. You learn by doing. I'll be your **advisor** for the first year, if you want."

Minh asked for a week to think. He talked to Hana, to the Parkers, to his parents. Everyone said yes. "There's no **upside** to saying no," said Tom. "You'd regret it forever."

He said yes.

The announcement was made at a staff meeting. People clapped. Ellie hugged him. Raj shook his hand. There was no **hype**, no big party: Grace hated that. Just a cake from The Blue Cup and a lot of coffee.

But first, there was work. A big story arrived the same week. An employee at a local factory contacted the Gazette. She said there had been serious **harassment** at the company: managers bullying and shouting at workers, especially young women. She had complained, but the company did nothing.

Minh and Ellie started a **probe**. They talked to twenty workers. The stories were **stark** and similar: the same two managers, the same behaviour, for years. The company had a **clause** in its contracts that stopped workers from talking publicly. "It's a culture of silence," said one woman. "Everyone knows. Nobody speaks."

The company's lawyers tried to stop the story. They said it was against their **disclosure** rules. Grace was calm. "Public interest is more important than their contract," she said. "We publish."

Before publishing, Minh gave the company a chance to respond. The CEO's **sentiment** was defensive at first. Then the evidence was too strong. A week after the story, the two managers were suspended. The company made a public **pledge** to change its culture and opened a new, independent complaint system.

"Your first big story as editor," said Grace. "And you're not editor yet."

There were other stories that month. A small **outbreak** of flu closed two schools. A fire at a warehouse was caused by a **spark** from old electrical machinery; investigators said it was a fire **hazard** that had been ignored for years. The council's new **thermal** camera, used to find heat leaks in houses, showed that half of Riverford's homes wasted energy. A local **lobby** group pushed the council to give grants for insulation.

There was also international news that touched Riverford. A **coup** in a small country abroad had forced some families to leave. A few arrived in Riverford as refugees. Minh interviewed one father, who said his **tribal** group had been attacked. "We left everything," he said. "But here we are safe."

In his last weeks as the old Minh, the reporter, he did one more interview: with Grace herself, for the podcast. He asked her about her **doctrine**, her rules for journalism.

"Three rules," she said. "One: check everything. Two: protect your sources. Three: don't be afraid of anyone. The **sheer** power of truth is enough."

"What will you do when you retire?"

"Garden. Read. Take care of my father." She smiled. "And listen to your podcast, to check you're doing it right."

"Will I be a good editor?" Minh asked, off the microphone.

Grace looked at him. "You'll make mistakes. You'll **bump** into problems. Some days you'll want to quit. But you'll be good. You have the most important thing."

"What's that?"

"You care about people more than stories. That's rare. Don't lose it."

When Minh got home, Hana had made dinner. Rose Mai was trying to crawl. Minh sat on the floor and watched her. He felt the **void** of Grace's coming absence, and the weight of the job. But he also felt something else: excitement.

"Editor of the Riverford Gazette," said Hana, raising a glass. "Not bad for a boy from a small town."

"Not bad," said Minh. "Now I just need to **gauge** whether I can do it."

"You can," said Hana. "I've never doubted it."

The Gazette was a small **affiliate** of a national network of local papers. Its contract with the network gave it convenience but not much money. Every **buck** mattered. The consultation with staff about the future would start soon. Opinions would differ. That was fine.
